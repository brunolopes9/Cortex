Mortgage interest rate in Portugal rises to 3.231% in October: what it means for your payment

Updated 2026-11-26 · 6 min read

The mortgage interest rate in Portugal rose for the fifth month in a row, reaching 3.231% in October, the highest level since August 2025, according to Statistics Portugal (INE). The average monthly payment climbed to €427, and more than half of it now goes on interest. Here is why, with a simple example, and what borrowers can do.

The mortgage interest rate in Portugal went up again in October 2026. Figures released by Statistics Portugal (INE) on 19 November show that the implied interest rate across all home loan contracts rose from 3.194% in September to 3.231% in October, an increase of 3.7 basis points. It is the fifth consecutive monthly rise and the highest reading since August 2025.

For households, that means an average monthly instalment of €427: €4 more than in September and €33 more than in October 2025. One detail stands out. Interest made up 50.8% of the average payment, up from 50.4% a month earlier. In other words, more than half of what borrowers pay the bank each month is, once again, interest rather than debt repayment.

It helps to know what the implied rate actually measures. It is not the rate on any single loan, nor the rate a bank would quote a new customer today. It is an average of the interest actually being paid on every home loan in force that month. That is why it moves slowly: it reflects, with a lag, what has already happened to Euribor.

Why are mortgage payments going up?

Most home loans in Portugal carry a variable or mixed rate. On the variable part, the rate you pay is made of two pieces:

  • The index, usually 3, 6 or 12-month Euribor, which moves with the market;
  • The spread, the bank's margin, which stays fixed for the life of the loan unless renegotiated.

According to Portuguese business outlet ECO, the rise in mortgage rates follows the increase in Euribor over recent months. That increase is linked to a change of course at the European Central Bank (ECB), which has gone back to raising interest rates to bring euro area inflation under control.

Because each loan is only reset on its scheduled date (every 3, 6 or 12 months, depending on the Euribor tenor), the impact reaches households gradually. That is why the implied rate keeps creeping up month after month even when Euribor itself is moving less: there are always loans hitting their reset date.

Why does interest make up more than half of the payment?

A mortgage payment has two parts: interest and capital repayment. Interest is charged on the balance still owed. When the rate rises, the interest slice gets bigger and, if the payment does not rise by the same proportion, less is left over to reduce the debt.

Using the INE numbers, the maths is straightforward. Of an average payment of €427, roughly €217 went on interest and roughly €210 on capital. A year earlier, with lower rates, the capital share was larger.

What about new loans?

For contracts signed in the last three months, INE says the rate rose from 2.959% to 2.994%. That is below the overall average, yet the average payment is much higher, at €759, up 14% year on year. The reason is that these loans are at the very beginning, with almost all the capital still outstanding, and new borrowers generally take out larger amounts than older loans did.

What could happen in the coming months?

Nobody knows for sure. The path of mortgage rates depends mainly on Euribor, which in turn reflects expectations about ECB decisions. The ECB's next monetary policy meeting is in December, and its decision will depend on the inflation and growth data that come in before then.

Two points are worth keeping in mind:

  • Even if Euribor levels off, the implied rate may keep rising for a few more months as remaining loans reach their reset date.
  • Likewise, if Euribor falls, the relief will also arrive with a delay.

What can mortgage holders do?

There is no one-size-fits-all answer, but a few sensible steps apply to most households:

  1. Read your contract. Know which Euribor tenor you use, when the next reset is and what your spread is.
  2. Stress-test your budget. Ask yourself whether you could absorb a rise of €40 or €50 a month.
  3. Shop around. Ask your bank to review the spread, or get offers from other banks to switch the loan, taking switching costs into account.
  4. Weigh a fixed or mixed rate. It brings predictability but may cost more if rates fall later.
  5. Consider an early repayment. It cuts future interest, but only makes sense once you have an emergency fund and have checked for any fees in your contract.
  6. Talk to the bank early. If you are at risk of falling behind, Portuguese banks have formal procedures for these cases, such as PERSI. The sooner you act, the more options you have.

Risks and things to watch

Each of these choices involves trade-offs. Fixing the rate protects you from rises but rules out the benefit of future cuts. Paying down capital reduces the debt but leaves less cash for emergencies. Switching banks can pay off, but it involves costs and paperwork.

It is also important not to try to offset a higher mortgage payment by chasing high-risk investments. Products promising quick returns can lead to losses and make things worse. The priority is a balanced budget and a cushion for the unexpected.

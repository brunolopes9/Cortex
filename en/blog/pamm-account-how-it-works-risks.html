PAMM Account Explained: How It Works, Fees, Checks and Risks

Updated 2026-11-12 · 10 min read

A PAMM account (Percentage Allocation Management Module) lets you put money with a broker and have a professional manager trade it for you. Your share of any profit or loss matches your share of the pooled capital, and the manager usually earns a performance fee. This guide covers how PAMM, MAM and copy trading work, how they differ from a fund, what to check first and the risks involved.

What is a PAMM account?

A PAMM account is an arrangement offered by some brokers, mostly in forex and CFDs, where one trader (the manager) trades money on behalf of several investors. PAMM stands for Percentage Allocation Management Module. The name describes the mechanism: each investor owns a percentage of the total capital under management, and every gain or loss is split in those percentages.

What sets a PAMM account apart is who holds the money. You open an account with the broker, deposit into it and allocate funds to a chosen manager. The broker keeps the money. The manager gets permission to trade it but normally cannot withdraw it. Withdrawals go back through the broker to the investor.

PAMM sits inside a wider group of managed account and social trading models, which includes MAM (Multi-Account Manager), LAMM (Lot Allocation Management Module) and copy trading. They share one idea: someone else makes the trading decisions while your money stays at a broker, not with the trader.

How a PAMM account works, step by step

Platforms differ in the details, but the usual process looks like this:

  1. The manager opens a PAMM master account with the broker and usually puts in some of their own capital. Many brokers require this, so the manager has money at stake too.
  2. The manager publishes an offer. This sets out the performance fee, any management fee, the minimum investment, trading or lock-up periods and withdrawal rules.
  3. You open an investor account with the same broker, deposit funds and allocate them to the manager's offer.
  4. The manager trades the combined capital as one pool. They do not see or handle your individual money.
  5. Results are split by percentage. If you supply 10% of the pool, you get 10% of every profit and loss.
  6. Fees are settled at set intervals (weekly, monthly or quarterly, for example). The broker works out the manager's performance fee and moves it automatically.
  7. You withdraw through the broker, following the offer's rules on timing and notice periods.

A simple worked example

The numbers below are round and purely hypothetical.

A manager runs a PAMM pool of 100,000 in total. You invest 10,000, so you own 10% of the pool. The performance fee is 20% of profits.

  • Month 1: the pool rises 5%, from 100,000 to 105,000. Your share of the gain is 500. The manager's 20% fee is 100, so your net profit is 400 and your balance is 10,400.
  • Month 2: the pool falls 10%. Your balance drops by 1,040 to 9,360. No performance fee is charged in a losing period.
  • Month 3: the pool rises 8%. Your balance goes up by about 749 to roughly 10,109. Under a high-water mark, the manager only earns a fee on gains above your previous peak of 10,400. You are still below that level, so no performance fee is due.

This example shows two things. First, losses come straight off your capital. Second, how the fee is calculated (especially whether there is a high-water mark) makes a real difference to what you end up with.

PAMM vs MAM vs LAMM: what is the difference?

People often use these terms as if they mean the same thing, but they describe different ways of allocating trades.

PAMM (Percentage Allocation Management Module)

Investor money is grouped into one pool for trading, and results are shared by percentage. Investors usually cannot change the risk level for their own portion. Everyone gets the same percentage result, before fees.

MAM (Multi-Account Manager)

With MAM, each investor keeps a separate account in their own name at the broker. The manager places trades from a master account, and software spreads them across the linked accounts by a set rule: equity proportion, a fixed multiplier or set lot sizes, for example. Some MAM setups let an investor pick a higher or lower risk multiplier, so two investors with the same manager can get different results.

LAMM (Lot Allocation Management Module)

LAMM gives every linked account the same trade size, whatever its balance. A smaller account therefore takes proportionally more risk on each trade. Fewer platforms offer it today, but you will still see the term.

Copy trading in brief

Copy trading links your own account to a trader's account. When they open or close a position, the platform does the same in your account, scaled to the amount you have chosen. You can usually stop copying at any time and set your own limits. The trade-off is execution differences. Your fills can differ from the provider's because of timing, slippage and minimum trade sizes, and the gap tends to be bigger on smaller accounts.

How a fund is different

When you buy into an investment fund, you hand money to a separate legal vehicle and get units or shares in return. A regulated fund typically comes with rules on custody, reporting and disclosure. With a PAMM or MAM account, the protections you get depend largely on the broker and where it is regulated, not on any fund rules. In some jurisdictions, managing money for others counts as portfolio management that needs its own authorisation. Rules vary between countries and change over time, so check what applies to you.

How PAMM managers are paid: profit share and performance fees

Most PAMM, MAM and similar arrangements run on a profit-share model. The manager takes a percentage of profits and, in many cases, nothing in a losing period. This is meant to align the manager's interests with yours, but read the terms carefully.

  • Performance fee: a percentage of profit for each settlement period.
  • High-water mark: fees are only charged on new profits above your previous peak, so you do not pay twice for making back the same losses. Check that it applies.
  • Management fee: some offers charge a percentage of capital whether or not they make money.
  • Entry or exit fees: less common, but they exist.
  • Trading costs: spreads, commissions and overnight financing (swap) charges come out of the account and lower returns. A high-trading strategy can build up large costs.

Profit share has a known downside. Because the manager shares in gains but does not pay for losses, they may be tempted to take bigger risks, particularly when the account is below its high-water mark. A track record that shows how returns were made matters as much as the returns themselves.

What to check before choosing a PAMM account

Use this checklist before you allocate any money.

1. The broker and its regulation

Under a PAMM or MAM arrangement, the broker holds your money, so it is the most important party. Look for:

  • Which authority regulates the broker, and in which country. Check the licence on the regulator's own public register, not just on the broker's website.
  • Whether client money is held in segregated accounts, separate from the broker's own money.
  • Whether there is an investor compensation scheme, and what it covers. Many schemes cover broker failure, not trading losses.
  • Whether the broker provides negative balance protection.
  • Which entity of the broker you are actually signing up with. Large groups often run several entities under different regulators, and protections differ between them.

2. The manager's track record, including drawdowns

A high return figure on its own tells you very little. Look for:

  • Length of history: a few good months does not make a strategy. Longer records that cover different market conditions are more informative.
  • Maximum drawdown: the biggest fall from a peak to a low. A strategy that gained 40% but once fell 50% along the way carries far more risk than the headline suggests.
  • Consistency: steady results, or a few big wins hiding long periods of losses?
  • Signs of dangerous techniques: martingale (doubling up after losses), grid trading without stop-losses, or never closing losing positions. These can produce smooth equity curves for a long time and then sudden, severe losses.
  • Verified data: results shown directly from the broker's platform are more reliable than screenshots or self-reported figures.
  • Capital under management: a strategy that worked on a small account may not work on a much bigger one.

3. Fees and how they are calculated

Confirm the performance fee rate, how often it is settled, whether there is a high-water mark, and any management, entry or exit fees. Ask whether trading costs are the broker's standard rates or include a mark-up.

4. Withdrawal rules and lock-up periods

  • Is there a minimum investment period?
  • Can you withdraw at any time, or only at the end of a trading interval?
  • Is there a fee or penalty for early withdrawal?
  • What happens to open positions when you withdraw? Some systems close your share at market prices, which may lock in a loss.

5. Your own control and transparency

Find out whether you can see positions in real time, set a loss limit that automatically disconnects you from the manager, and get clear statements. The more you can see, the easier it is to spot problems early.

The risks of PAMM accounts

A PAMM account does not remove trading risk. It hands the decisions to someone else. The main risks are:

  • Market and leverage risk: forex and CFDs are usually traded with leverage, which magnifies both gains and losses. You can lose a large part, or all, of the money you allocate.
  • Manager risk: the manager may change style, take on more risk or simply perform badly. Past results do not guarantee future ones.
  • Broker risk: if the broker fails or acts improperly, your money is exposed. Protection depends on its regulation and on any compensation scheme.
  • Liquidity and withdrawal risk: lock-up periods and settlement intervals can delay getting your money out, sometimes just when you most want to.
  • Cost drag: fees and trading costs keep coming, and they lower net returns even in good periods.
  • Scams and impersonation: fraudsters sometimes pose as PAMM managers or invent broker platforms. Be wary of guaranteed or fixed monthly returns, pressure to decide quickly, requests to send money to a personal account or wallet, and offers from unsolicited messages on social media.
  • Complexity: leveraged products are complex. Make sure you understand how losses can build up before you take part.

As a general rule, only allocate money you can afford to lose, and do not put everything with one manager or one broker.

Is a PAMM account right for you?

A PAMM or MAM account may suit you if you want exposure to an active trading strategy, do not want to trade yourself, and accept the high risk that comes with leveraged markets. It is less suitable if you need the money soon, cannot accept large swings in value, or want the protections of a regulated fund.

Key takeaways

  • A PAMM account pools investor capital at a broker so one manager can trade it, and results are shared by percentage.
  • MAM keeps a separate account for each investor. Copy trading copies trades into your own account with more control for you.
  • Managers are usually paid a performance fee. A high-water mark protects you from paying twice on the same gains.
  • Check the broker's regulation, the manager's full track record including drawdowns, all fees and the withdrawal rules.
  • Losses can be large. Leverage, manager behaviour and broker risk all matter.

Frequently asked questions

What does PAMM stand for?

PAMM stands for Percentage Allocation Management Module. It is a broker system that lets one manager trade pooled investor capital, with profits and losses split according to each investor's percentage of the pool.

Is a PAMM account safe?

No PAMM account is risk-free. The manager usually cannot withdraw your money, but you can still lose a large part of it through trading losses, especially with leverage. How safe your money is also depends on how well the broker is regulated and whether client funds are held separately from the broker's own money.

What is the difference between PAMM and copy trading?

In a PAMM account, investor money is pooled and traded as one by the manager. In copy trading, the provider's trades are copied into your own separate account. You usually have more control there, such as choosing the copy size, setting loss limits and stopping at any time.

How do PAMM managers get paid?

Most charge a performance fee, which is a percentage of the profits made in each settlement period. Some also charge a management fee. A high-water mark means the manager is only paid on new profits above the account's previous peak.

Can I withdraw money from a PAMM account at any time?

It depends on the offer. Many PAMM accounts have lock-up periods or only process withdrawals at the end of a trading interval, and some charge early withdrawal fees. Check the rules before you invest.

What is a MAM account?

A MAM (Multi-Account Manager) account lets a manager trade several separate investor accounts from one master account. Each investor keeps an individual account at the broker, and trades are allocated by a set rule, sometimes with a risk multiplier the investor can choose.

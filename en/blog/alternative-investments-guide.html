Alternative Investments: A Practical Guide Beyond Stocks and Bonds

Updated 2026-11-14 · 10 min read

Alternative investments are assets outside traditional stocks, bonds and cash, such as property, gold, crypto, private equity, peer-to-peer lending and managed or algorithmic strategies. They can add diversification, but usually come with lower liquidity, higher fees and less transparency. This guide explains each option, how they compare and how to think about how much to allocate.

What are alternative investments?

Alternative investments are any assets that sit outside the three traditional building blocks of a portfolio: listed shares, bonds and cash. The term covers a wide and very mixed group, from a buy-to-let flat or a gold coin to a stake in a private company, a crypto token or a fund run by an algorithm.

What they have in common is not a single risk profile but a set of characteristics. They are often harder to buy and sell, harder to value, more expensive to hold and less tightly standardised than a mainstream index fund. Some are also less regulated, or not regulated at all.

That does not make them good or bad. It means they need a different kind of homework before you commit money.

Why investors look beyond stocks and bonds

People usually consider alternatives for one or more of these reasons:

  • Diversification: some alternatives have historically behaved differently from shares and bonds, so they may soften the swings of a portfolio. This relationship is not fixed and can break down in a crisis, when many assets fall together.
  • Inflation protection: real assets such as property and commodities are often seen as a partial hedge against rising prices, although the link is imperfect and varies over time.
  • Income: rents, loan interest and private credit can provide regular cash flow.
  • Access to different return sources: private companies, new technologies or systematic trading strategies offer exposures you cannot easily get through a standard index fund.

The trade-off is that you usually give up some combination of liquidity, simplicity and low cost. Understanding that trade-off is the core of investing in alternatives sensibly.

The main types of alternative investments

Real estate

Property is the most familiar alternative. You can own it directly, for example a rental flat, or indirectly through listed real estate investment trusts (REITs), property funds or crowdfunding platforms.

Direct property can generate rental income and capital growth, but it is lumpy, illiquid and labour-intensive. Buying and selling involve legal fees, taxes such as stamp duty in the UK, and agent costs, and a sale can take months. Concentration is a real risk: one property in one area is a single, large bet. Using a mortgage adds leverage, which magnifies both gains and losses.

REITs trade on stock exchanges, so they are far easier to buy and sell and allow small amounts. The catch is that their prices move with the stock market day to day and can be volatile, even if the underlying buildings change value slowly.

Commodities and gold

Commodities include energy (oil, gas), metals (gold, silver, copper) and agricultural products. Most private investors gain exposure through exchange-traded products rather than holding physical barrels or bushels.

Gold deserves its own mention. It is widely held as a store of value and is often bought in periods of uncertainty. However, gold pays no income, can go through long periods of flat or falling prices, and physical bullion involves storage and insurance costs plus a spread between buying and selling prices.

Many commodity funds track futures contracts rather than the physical asset. When contracts are rolled forward, costs or gains arise that can make the fund’s return differ noticeably from the headline spot price. Read how a product gains its exposure before buying it.

Cryptocurrencies

Crypto assets such as Bitcoin and Ether are a newer and highly speculative alternative. Prices have experienced very large rises and falls, sometimes losing a large share of their value within months. Crypto generates no cash flow in the traditional sense, so valuation depends heavily on adoption and sentiment.

Beyond price risk there is custody and platform risk. Exchanges and lending platforms have failed, the collapse of FTX in 2022 being a well-known example, leaving customers unable to access funds. Lost private keys usually mean lost coins. In many countries, including the UK, crypto holdings are not protected by deposit or investor compensation schemes. Regulators commonly warn that investors should be prepared to lose all the money they put in.

Private equity and venture capital

Private equity means owning shares in companies that are not listed on a stock exchange, from mature businesses bought out by funds to early-stage start-ups backed by venture capital or angel investors.

Traditional private equity funds often lock in investors’ money for many years, commonly around a decade, and charge management fees plus a share of profits. Valuations are reported infrequently and are estimates rather than market prices, which can make returns look smoother than the true risk.

Retail routes include listed private equity trusts, venture capital trusts in the UK and equity crowdfunding. Early-stage investing in particular carries a high chance that individual companies fail completely, and shares can be very difficult to sell.

Peer-to-peer (P2P) lending

P2P platforms let you lend money to individuals or businesses, receiving interest in return. Advertised rates are typically higher than savings accounts because you are taking credit risk: borrowers can default.

Key points to understand:

  • P2P loans are not savings accounts. In the UK they are not covered by the FSCS deposit protection that applies to bank savings.
  • Getting money out early may depend on finding another lender to buy your loans, which can be slow or impossible in a downturn.
  • Platform failure is a separate risk from borrower default; check what happens to your loans if the platform closes.
  • Defaults tend to rise in recessions, exactly when you might want your cash.

Managed and algorithmic strategies

This group includes hedge funds, managed futures and systematic or algorithmic trading, where rules-based software decides when to buy and sell. Some strategies aim to profit in rising and falling markets, which is why they are sometimes used as diversifiers.

Historically, hedge funds were mostly limited to wealthy or professional investors and often charged both a management fee and a performance fee. Today there are also retail-accessible services. Some, such as Cortex Automation, run an automated strategy while the client’s money stays in their own broker account, with fees linked to profits rather than a fixed charge on assets.

Whatever the structure, the important questions are the same: what the strategy actually does, how it has behaved in bad markets, whether it uses leverage, how large past drawdowns were, and who holds your money. Backtests and good past performance can look impressive but do not guarantee future results, and algorithmic strategies can lose money, sometimes quickly.

Other alternatives

Collectibles such as art, wine, watches and classic cars, as well as farmland, forestry and infrastructure, also count as alternatives. They can be enjoyable to own but usually have high transaction costs, wide price uncertainty and markets that rely on specialist knowledge.

The key risks of alternative investments

Liquidity risk

Liquidity is how quickly you can turn an asset into cash at a fair price. Many alternatives are slow to sell, have lock-up periods or can suspend withdrawals in stressed markets. Never put money you might need soon into something you cannot easily exit.

Fees and costs

Costs are often higher and less visible than with index funds. Look for management fees, performance fees, entry and exit charges, spreads, storage and platform fees. A small difference in annual cost compounds into a large difference over many years.

Valuation and transparency

If an asset is not traded daily, its reported value is an estimate. That can hide volatility and make it hard to know what your holding is really worth until you sell.

Regulation and counterparty risk

Protection varies enormously. Some products are fully regulated; others sit outside any compensation scheme. Always check who holds your assets, whether client money is segregated, and what happens if the provider fails.

Leverage and complexity

Borrowing, derivatives and complex structures can amplify losses beyond what you expect. If you cannot explain how an investment makes money and how it could lose money, it is a warning sign.

Fraud

Alternatives attract scams, particularly in crypto, property schemes and anything promising high, steady or guaranteed returns. Legitimate investments do not guarantee profits.

How much should you allocate to alternative investments?

There is no universal correct figure. The right amount depends on your goals, time horizon, existing savings, income stability and, crucially, how much loss you could tolerate without it affecting your life or forcing you to sell.

A sensible order of priorities for most people is:

  1. Build an emergency fund in easily accessible cash.
  2. Deal with expensive debt.
  3. Set up a core, diversified portfolio, for example low-cost funds holding shares and bonds, appropriate to your goals.
  4. Only then consider alternatives as a satellite around that core.

Many investors keep alternatives as a minority of their portfolio, and keep the most speculative assets, such as crypto or early-stage start-ups, to a small slice they could afford to lose entirely.

A simple worked example

Imagine a hypothetical investor with £20,000 to invest after building an emergency fund. One way they might structure it, purely to illustrate the thinking:

  • £16,000 (80%) in a diversified core of share and bond funds.
  • £2,000 (10%) in a REIT fund and a gold ETF for real-asset exposure.
  • £1,000 (5%) in a managed or algorithmic strategy, after checking its structure and history.
  • £1,000 (5%) in crypto, treated as money that could go to zero.

If the crypto holding fell by 80%, the loss would be £800, or 4% of the total portfolio. Painful, but not life-changing. If instead the investor had put £10,000 into crypto, the same fall would cost £8,000, or 40% of their money. Position size, not just asset choice, determines how much damage a bad outcome can do. These figures are an illustration, not a recommendation.

How to evaluate an alternative investment

Before committing money, work through a short checklist:

  • What exactly do I own? A physical asset, a share in a fund, a loan, a token or a contract?
  • Where is my money held? In my own account, segregated with a custodian, or pooled on a platform?
  • How do I get out? Notice periods, lock-ups, exit fees and what happens in a crisis.
  • What are all the costs? Ask for a full breakdown, including performance fees.
  • What is the worst historical loss? Look at drawdowns, not just average returns.
  • Is it regulated, and by whom? Verify independently rather than relying on marketing material.
  • Does it fit my plan? Does it genuinely diversify what I already hold, or just add risk?

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Chasing whatever has risen most recently.
  • Putting too much into a single illiquid asset.
  • Ignoring fees because headline returns look high.
  • Confusing high advertised yields with low risk.
  • Investing in something you do not understand because others are.
  • Forgetting about tax, which differs between asset types and countries.

The bottom line

Alternative investments can broaden a portfolio and provide exposures that stocks and bonds alone do not offer. They also bring less liquidity, higher costs, more complexity and, in some cases, a real risk of losing everything. Used thoughtfully, sized carefully and understood properly, they can play a supporting role around a solid core rather than replacing it.

Frequently asked questions

What counts as an alternative investment?

Any asset outside traditional listed shares, bonds and cash, such as property, gold and commodities, cryptocurrencies, private equity, peer-to-peer lending, hedge funds, algorithmic strategies and collectibles.

Are alternative investments riskier than stocks?

Not always, but many are less liquid, harder to value, more expensive and less protected. Some, such as crypto and early-stage start-ups, can be considerably more volatile and may lose all their value.

How much of my portfolio should be in alternative investments?

There is no single right answer. Many investors keep alternatives as a minority alongside a diversified core, and limit speculative assets to an amount they could afford to lose entirely.

Is peer-to-peer lending protected like a savings account?

No. In the UK, P2P lending is not covered by FSCS deposit protection. You can lose money if borrowers default or the platform fails, and withdrawals may be slow.

Is gold a good alternative investment?

Gold is widely used as a store of value and diversifier, but it pays no income, can fall or stagnate for long periods, and physical gold involves storage and dealing costs.

Can algorithmic trading strategies lose money?

Yes. Automated strategies can suffer losses and drawdowns, especially if they use leverage or market conditions change. Strong backtests or past results do not guarantee future performance.

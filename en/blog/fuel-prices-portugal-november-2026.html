Fuel prices in Portugal fall on 16 November: what it means for you

Updated 2026-11-21 · 6 min read

Fuel prices in Portugal are coming down again from Monday 16 November: the Portuguese automobile club ACP estimates a cut of about 6 cents per litre for diesel and 2 cents for petrol. The government is keeping part of that relief by trimming its temporary fuel tax discount. Here are the numbers, what you actually save, and what it means for inflation and investors.

Fuel prices in Portugal: how much are they falling?

Fuel prices in mainland Portugal are expected to fall from Monday 16 November. Estimates published on Friday by ACP (Automóvel Club de Portugal) pointed to a drop of around 6 cents per litre for diesel and around 2 cents for petrol.

According to the public broadcaster RTP, using data from DGEG (the Portuguese energy and geology authority), the average reference price should be close to €2.06 per litre for standard diesel and €1.89 for standard petrol. These are averages. Every station sets its own price, and the gap between brands and towns can be several cents.

This is the second week in a row of lower prices. The previous week's cut had already been described by Diário de Notícias as the largest in more than four years.

Why did the fuel tax discount shrink?

On Friday 13 November, the government published Order (Portaria) no. 501-A/2026/1 in the official gazette, changing once again the temporary discount on ISP, Portugal's tax on petroleum products. The discount gets smaller, which means the tax goes up slightly.

  • Diesel: the discount falls from about 9.20 to 8.18 cents per litre.
  • Petrol: the discount falls from about 3.91 to 3.76 cents per litre.

The logic is straightforward. When pre-tax prices fall, the government withdraws part of the support it introduced when prices were rising. As a result, not all of the drop in international prices reaches the pump.

Press calculations do not match to the cent. Some reports put the net relief for diesel at about 6 cents after the tax adjustment, while others work it out at under 5 cents. The exact figure is only visible on the price board at each station. The discount applies only to diesel and unleaded petrol sold on the mainland.

How are fuel prices set in Portugal?

The price at the pump is made up of several building blocks:

  1. International prices for refined products, meaning diesel and petrol after refining. These follow crude oil, demand and refinery capacity.
  2. The euro to dollar exchange rate. Oil trades in dollars, so a weaker euro makes fuel more expensive even if the barrel price does not move.
  3. Taxes. ISP is a fixed amount per litre, and 23% VAT is charged on top of everything, including the ISP itself.
  4. Logistics, blended biofuels and margins for distributors and stations.

Because Portuguese pump prices mainly track the previous week's international prices, changes reach drivers with a lag. That is why forecasts for the following week appear on Friday and new prices take effect on Monday.

In recent months, oil markets have been very sensitive to tensions in the Middle East. That helps explain why prices can swing by several cents from one week to the next.

How much do you actually save? A simple example

Take a hypothetical driver who uses 60 litres of diesel a month, for example 1,000 km at 6 litres per 100 km. If the price drops by 5 cents a litre, the saving is 60 × 0.05 = €3 a month. On a 50-litre tank, it is €2.50.

These figures are hypothetical and rounded. They put the headline in perspective: a cut of a few cents helps, but it does not transform a household budget. For people who drive a lot, such as professional drivers or businesses with vehicle fleets, the cumulative effect is more noticeable.

Often, comparing stations is more useful than waiting for the right Monday. The gap between the most and least expensive station in the same area can be bigger than a whole week's price change. The DGEG fuel price portal lets you make that comparison.

Do fuel prices affect inflation and Euribor?

Energy is one of the most volatile parts of inflation. Lower fuel prices tend to ease the inflation rate in the following months, and higher prices push it up. Statistics Portugal (INE) publishes these figures every month.

The ECB (European Central Bank) watches energy closely, but it pays a lot of attention to core inflation, which strips out energy and unprocessed food precisely because they jump around so much. One or two weeks of cheaper fuel are not enough on their own to change interest rate policy or Euribor, the benchmark rate behind most Portuguese mortgages. Only a sustained trend would carry that weight.

Is it worth investing in oil when prices fall?

News like this often makes people think about buying oil or energy shares. Before doing so, it helps to keep a few points in mind:

  • By the time the news is out, the market has already moved. The cut at the pump reflects last week's prices. Financial markets react in minutes, not days.
  • Oil is highly volatile. It can move several percent in a single day on a political statement, an inventory report or an OPEC+ decision.
  • Leveraged products magnify losses. CFDs and other leveraged products can make you gain or lose far more than the underlying price move, and many retail investors lose money with them.
  • An energy company is not the same as a barrel of oil. Its shares also depend on debt, refining margins, dividends and the wider stock market.

For most people, it makes more sense to treat energy as a small part of a diversified portfolio than as a standalone bet based on a headline.

What are the risks for the coming weeks?

This week's cut does not guarantee further cuts. Several factors could reverse the trend:

  • fresh tensions in the Middle East or other producing regions;
  • a weaker euro against the dollar;
  • higher demand for heating oil during the European winter;
  • further changes to the ISP discount, which the government has been adjusting almost weekly.

What each driver can control is their own side of the equation: compare prices, drive more efficiently and avoid budgeting around a single week.

Summary

  • From 16 November, diesel should fall by about 6 cents and petrol by about 2 cents, according to ACP estimates.
  • The government has trimmed its temporary ISP discount, which absorbs part of the cut.
  • Average reference prices are around €2.06/L for diesel and €1.89/L for petrol, but they vary from station to station.
  • The effect on household budgets is modest; comparing stations usually saves more.

Frequently asked questions

How much is diesel falling in Portugal this week?

According to ACP estimates published on 13 November, diesel should fall by about 6 cents per litre from 16 November, to an average reference price close to €2.06. Individual stations may charge more or less.

Why might the cut at the pump be smaller than forecast?

Because the government reduced its temporary discount on ISP, the fuel tax, through Order no. 501-A/2026/1. For diesel, the discount drops from about 9.20 to 8.18 cents per litre, absorbing part of the fall in market prices.

Does the ISP discount apply in the Azores and Madeira?

No. The temporary ISP discount applies to diesel and unleaded petrol sold in mainland Portugal. The autonomous regions have their own rules and prices.

Will cheaper fuel bring Euribor down?

Not directly. It helps ease inflation, but the ECB (European Central Bank) looks at sustained trends and at core inflation. One or two weeks of lower fuel prices are not enough to change interest rates.

Is it worth investing in oil when fuel prices fall?

There is no single answer. Oil is very volatile, and by the time a cut reaches the pump the market has already reacted. Leveraged products such as CFDs can lead to large losses. For most people, energy should be a small part of a diversified portfolio.

Technical vs fundamental analysis: differences, examples and risks

Updated 2026-11-27 · 6 min read

Fundamental analysis tries to work out what an asset is worth; technical analysis reads the price chart to decide when to get in and out. In the technical vs fundamental analysis debate there is no single winner: it depends on your goal and time horizon, and many investors use both.

Technical vs fundamental analysis: what is the difference?

Anyone who starts investing soon runs into the technical vs fundamental analysis question. The short answer is that the two methods answer different questions. Fundamental analysis asks what to buy: what a company, currency or other asset is really worth. Technical analysis asks when to buy or sell: what price and volume behaviour suggest about timing.

They are not rival religions. Many long-term investors lean mostly on fundamentals, many short-term traders lean mostly on charts, and plenty of people mix the two. Neither one guarantees results, and both are wrong fairly often.

What is fundamental analysis?

Fundamental analysis tries to estimate an asset's intrinsic value, meaning what it ought to be worth based on its underlying economics. If the market price sits well below that estimate, the asset may be cheap; if it sits well above, it may be expensive.

For a listed company, this means looking at the business as if you were buying all of it: how much it sells, how much it earns, how much it owes and how fast it might grow.

Which metrics does fundamental analysis use?

  • Revenue and earnings: are they growing, flat or shrinking?
  • Price-to-earnings ratio (P/E): how many years of current profits the market is paying for each share.
  • Debt: a heavily indebted company becomes more fragile when interest rates rise.
  • Margins and cash flow: they show whether accounting profit turns into real cash.
  • Dividends: how much the company pays out and whether that is sustainable.
  • The wider economy: European Central Bank (ECB) interest rates, inflation, growth and the state of the sector.

It is an approach built mainly for months and years, not days.

What is technical analysis?

Technical analysis studies price and volume charts to spot trends, patterns and areas where the price has reacted before. It rests on a simple idea: much of what is known about an asset is already reflected in its price, and the way buyers and sellers behave tends to repeat.

It does not try to say what a company is worth. It tries to identify the more likely direction of price and, above all, to set clear points for entry, exit and the maximum loss you are willing to accept.

Which tools does technical analysis use?

  • Trend: a series of higher highs and higher lows (uptrend) or lower highs and lower lows (downtrend).
  • Support and resistance: levels where the price stopped falling or rising in the past.
  • Moving averages: the average price over, say, the last 50 or 200 days, used to filter out noise.
  • Momentum indicators such as RSI or MACD, which measure the strength of recent moves.
  • Volume: a move backed by heavy volume is usually seen as more meaningful.

A simple example: one share seen from both sides

Picture a hypothetical business, Company X, whose shares trade at 50 euros. The numbers are rounded and purely illustrative.

The fundamental view

Company X earns 2.50 euros per share each year. Divide 50 by 2.50 and the P/E is 20. If similar companies in the same sector trade on an average P/E of 15, Company X looks relatively expensive, unless it can grow faster than its peers.

If earnings climb to 4 euros per share over a few years and the market values it on a P/E of 15, the share would be worth around 60 euros. If earnings stall, 50 euros may be too much. The fundamental conclusion: buying only makes sense if there are solid reasons to believe in that growth.

The technical view

On the chart, the price is above its 200-day moving average, which is usually read as an uptrend. Over recent months the share has repeatedly bounced near 45 euros (support) and stalled near 55 euros (resistance).

A technical trader might set out a plan like this: buy at 50 euros, place a stop loss at 45 euros and target 60 euros if the 55-euro resistance gives way. That means risking 5 euros per share to try to make 10, a risk-reward ratio of 1 to 2. With 100 shares, the planned maximum loss would be about 500 euros (before costs and any sudden price gaps) and the potential gain 1,000 euros.

Putting the two together

Someone combining both methods could use fundamentals to decide whether they want Company X in their portfolio and the chart to decide when to buy and where to cap the loss. Even so, an unexpected piece of news, such as weaker-than-expected results, could push the price below 45 euros overnight.

Which is better, technical or fundamental analysis?

It depends on your goal and time horizon, not on which one is the “true” method.

  • Long-term investing (retirement, saving over many years): fundamentals tend to matter more, as does the simpler option of investing in a diversified way rather than picking individual shares.
  • Short-term trading: fundamentals barely change from one day to the next, so charts and risk management carry more weight.
  • A blended approach: common among active investors, who screen assets on fundamentals and time their trades with charts.

More important than the school you choose is having a consistent method, written rules and risk management that limits the damage from mistakes, because mistakes will happen.

Does it work for shares, forex, gold and bitcoin?

Technical analysis can be applied to any asset with a price history, which is why it is so widely used in forex, commodities and crypto.

Fundamental analysis has to be adapted to each market. In forex, it looks at interest rates, inflation and growth in each economy: decisions by the ECB and the US Federal Reserve, for example, move the euro against the dollar. For gold, real interest rates, the dollar and safe-haven demand matter most. For bitcoin, fundamental analysis is more debatable because there are no earnings or dividends; people tend to look at its capped supply, adoption and regulation.

What are the risks and limits of each approach?

  • False sense of certainty: neither a valuation model nor a tidy chart removes uncertainty.
  • Bad estimates: in fundamental analysis, small errors in growth forecasts change the estimated value a lot.
  • False signals: in technical analysis, breakouts fail and patterns often look obvious only in hindsight.
  • Over-optimisation: tweaking rules until they fit the past perfectly, which rarely carries into the future.
  • Costs: trading often means paying more in spreads and commissions.
  • Leverage: in products such as CFDs, it magnifies both gains and losses, whatever analysis you use.

How to get started without overcomplicating it

  1. Start by defining your goal and time horizon: long-term investing or active trading.
  2. Learn the basics of one approach properly before mixing the two.
  3. Write down simple rules: what makes you buy, what makes you sell and how much you are prepared to lose.
  4. Practise with small amounts or on a demo account.
  5. Keep a record of your decisions and review your mistakes calmly.

Frequently asked questions

Does technical analysis actually work?

There is no consensus. Some research finds little consistent edge, while other studies find effects in certain markets and periods. Where technical analysis tends to be most useful is in setting clear entry, exit and stop-loss rules, but it does not guarantee profits.

Can I use technical and fundamental analysis together?

Yes, and many investors do: fundamentals help choose the asset, and the chart helps choose the timing and the loss limit. What matters is setting the rules before you invest.

Which is easier for beginners?

The basics of technical analysis are quick to learn but hard to apply with discipline. Fundamental analysis requires reading accounts and understanding businesses. For long-term investors, the simplest route is often to diversify rather than pick individual shares.

Is fundamental analysis useful for short-term trading?

Only to a limited extent, because fundamentals change slowly. The exceptions are events such as earnings releases or central bank decisions, which can move prices sharply in a short time.

Do I need expensive software for technical or fundamental analysis?

No. Most brokers provide free charts and listed companies publish their accounts. Having a method, written rules and sound risk management matters far more than the software.

What Is Leverage in Trading and How Does It Work?

Updated 2026-11-14 · 6 min read

Leverage means using funds provided by your broker to open a position larger than the cash in your account. At 1:10, €1,000 controls a €10,000 position, so both profits and losses are calculated on the larger amount. It can be a useful tool, but it is also one of the quickest ways to lose money in the markets.

What is leverage in trading, in a nutshell?

If you are wondering what leverage in trading is, here is the short answer: it lets you open a position larger than the money you have in your account, with your broker covering the difference. With 1:10 leverage, for instance, €1,000 lets you control a €10,000 position.

The key point is that leverage does not change the market. It changes the size of your exposure. If the price moves your way, the gain is calculated on €10,000. If it moves against you, so is the loss. Leverage magnifies gains and losses by exactly the same factor.

The idea is not unique to trading. Anyone who buys a home with a mortgage is using leverage: they put down a deposit and the bank finances the rest. The difference is that financial markets move every day, so the consequences arrive much faster.

How does leverage work?

Leveraged trading usually happens through CFDs (contracts for difference) or futures. You do not pay the full value of the asset. Instead, you put up a deposit called margin, and the broker provides the rest of the exposure.

Leverage is quoted either as a ratio (1:5, 1:10, 1:30) or as a margin percentage. Both describe the same thing:

  • 1:5 means a margin of 20% of the position value.
  • 1:10 means a margin of 10%.
  • 1:30 means a margin of roughly 3.33%.

How do you calculate margin?

The formula is simple: margin = position size ÷ leverage. To open a hypothetical €20,000 position at 1:20, your broker asks for €1,000 of margin. The rest of your account balance is free, but it acts as a cushion to absorb losses while the trade is open.

If losses eat through that cushion, the broker may ask you to add funds (a margin call) or close the position automatically.

A simple example: €1,000 with and without leverage

Picture a purely hypothetical account with €1,000 and a stock index that can rise or fall. The table shows how different leverage levels affect your capital, before costs.

LeverageExposureMarket rises 2%Market falls 5%
No leverage (1:1)€1,000+€20 (+2%)−€50 (−5%)
1:5€5,000+€100 (+10%)−€250 (−25%)
1:10€10,000+€200 (+20%)−€500 (−50%)
1:30€30,000+€600 (+60%)−€1,500 (more than the account)

Look at the last row. At 1:30, a fall of just 5% would wipe out more than your entire capital. In practice the broker would close the position before that point, but the account would be close to empty.

The table also shows why leverage is so tempting: a small market move seems to turn into a big gain. The catch is that markets do not move in the direction we would like on demand, and the same arithmetic works in reverse.

Does leverage magnify gains and losses equally?

In percentage terms, yes. Leverage multiplies the result of a price move by the same factor, up or down. But there is an important asymmetry when it comes to recovering.

If you lose 50% of your account, a 50% gain will not get you back to where you started. If €1,000 drops to €500, you need another €500, which is a 100% gain on what is left. The higher the leverage, the easier it is to fall into losses that are hard to recover from.

Then there are costs. With CFDs, holding a leveraged position overnight usually means paying a financing charge (often called swap or overnight fee), calculated on the full position value, not just on your margin.

What are the leverage limits in Europe?

In the European Union, ESMA (the European Securities and Markets Authority) set caps on CFDs sold to retail clients, which national regulators enforce. The maximum levels are:

  • 1:30 for major currency pairs (such as EUR/USD).
  • 1:20 for other currency pairs, gold and major stock indices.
  • 1:10 for commodities other than gold and for minor stock indices.
  • 1:5 for individual shares and other assets.
  • 1:2 for cryptocurrencies such as bitcoin.

These are ceilings, not recommendations. Nothing forces you to use all the leverage a broker offers.

What happens when losses approach your margin?

The same EU rules require brokers to close positions once account equity falls to 50% of the required margin, and to offer negative balance protection. This means a retail client should not lose more than the money they deposited. They can, however, still lose all of it.

Bear in mind that brokers outside the EU, or accounts classified as professional, may offer much higher leverage with fewer safeguards.

What are the risks of leverage?

  • Magnified losses: an ordinary market move can become a large loss on your capital.
  • Forced closure: your position may be closed at the worst moment, even if the market later recovers.
  • Gaps: when a market opens far above or below its previous close, a stop loss may be filled at a worse price than expected.
  • Costs add up: spreads, commissions and overnight financing weigh more as exposure grows.
  • Emotional pressure: watching a balance swing sharply pushes many people into rushed decisions.

Is leverage worth using?

It depends on your goals and experience. For long-term investors, for example someone putting a fixed amount into index funds each month, leverage is usually unnecessary and adds a risk that can undo years of discipline.

For active traders, it can be a useful capital management tool, as long as it is moderate and tied to clear risk rules. What matters is not how much leverage is available, but how much of your capital is at risk on each trade.

How to use leverage more carefully

  1. Start from the maximum loss, not the gain: decide how much you accept losing per trade, say 1% of the account, and size the position from there.
  2. Always use a stop loss: without one, a leveraged position is exposed to losses that grow quickly.
  3. Prefer low leverage: 1:2 or 1:5 leaves far more room for error than 1:30.
  4. Factor in costs: include overnight financing if you plan to hold positions for several days.
  5. Practise on a demo account: seeing how margin behaves without real money avoids nasty surprises.
  6. Only use money you can afford to lose: never your emergency fund or money needed for bills.

Summary

Leverage means controlling a position larger than your own capital, with help from your broker. It magnifies gains and losses equally, carries costs and can lead to positions being closed for you. In Europe, retail clients benefit from leverage caps and negative balance protection, but can still lose everything they deposit. Used sparingly and with risk rules, leverage is a tool; used to the maximum, it is mostly a source of risk.

Frequently asked questions

What does 1:100 leverage mean?

It means your position is 100 times larger than the margin you put up, so the margin is 1%. A move of just 1% against you is enough to wipe out that margin. In the EU, this level is not allowed on CFDs for retail clients.

Can I lose more than I deposited?

Retail clients of EU-regulated brokers benefit from negative balance protection, so losses are limited to the account balance. With brokers outside the EU or on professional accounts, that protection may not apply.

What is the best leverage for beginners?

There is no single right number. For beginners, the most cautious approach is low or no leverage, practising on a demo account and limiting the risk on each trade to a small share of capital.

What is a margin call?

It is a warning from your broker that your account equity no longer covers the margin required by your open positions. If you do not add funds or reduce positions, the broker may close them automatically.

Is leveraged trading legal in Europe?

Yes. It is allowed, but CFDs sold to retail clients are subject to EU-wide caps, ranging from 1:30 on major currency pairs to 1:2 on cryptocurrencies.

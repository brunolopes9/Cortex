Investing in Gold and Bitcoin: How Two Very Different Assets Can Work Together

Updated 2026-11-13 · 9 min read

Investing in gold and bitcoin together can spread your exposure across two assets that respond to different forces. Gold has a long history as a safe haven and is moved by the dollar, interest rates and geopolitics. Bitcoin is a young, highly volatile asset driven largely by liquidity and risk appetite. This guide explains how they differ, what diversification across them can and cannot do, and why one automated strategy recently added gold alongside bitcoin.

Investing in gold and bitcoin: the short answer

People searching for investing in gold and bitcoin usually want to know whether it makes sense to hold or trade both, rather than picking one. The short answer is that the two assets are very different. Gold is thousands of years old and widely held by central banks. Bitcoin has existed since 2009 and is still finding its place in the financial system.

Because they are driven by different things, combining them can reduce your dependence on any single market. It does not remove risk. Both assets can fall, sometimes at the same time. And trading either one with leverage can turn small price moves into large losses.

The rest of this article explains the differences in practical terms. It also covers what diversification really achieves and why an automated trading strategy might add a second, unrelated asset to its portfolio.

How gold and bitcoin differ

Both are sometimes called a store of value, and both are often quoted against the US dollar (XAU/USD for gold, BTC/USD for bitcoin). That is about where the similarities end.

Volatility: how much the price moves

Bitcoin has historically been far more volatile than gold. Double-digit percentage moves in a single week are not unusual for bitcoin, and drawdowns of more than half its value from a peak have happened several times. Gold moves too, sometimes sharply, but its typical daily and monthly swings have historically been much smaller.

This matters for position sizing. A position of the same size in each asset carries very different risk. Many traders and strategies adjust the size of their trades to reflect each asset's volatility instead of putting in equal amounts of money.

What moves the price

Gold tends to respond to:

  • Real interest rates: gold pays no interest, so higher real yields make holding it relatively less attractive, and falling real yields often support it.
  • The US dollar: because gold is priced in dollars, a stronger dollar has often coincided with weaker gold prices, although this relationship is not fixed.
  • Geopolitics and uncertainty: wars, political crises and financial stress often lead to demand for gold as a safe haven.
  • Central bank and physical demand: purchases by central banks, jewellery demand and investment in bars, coins and exchange-traded funds.
  • Inflation expectations: gold is widely seen as a long-term hedge against the loss of purchasing power, although over shorter periods the link is inconsistent.

Bitcoin tends to respond to:

  • Global liquidity and risk appetite: in periods of easy money and optimism, bitcoin has often risen with other speculative assets, and it has often fallen sharply when liquidity tightens.
  • Regulation and access: news about rules, exchange approvals, bans or new investment products can move the price strongly.
  • Its fixed supply schedule: new issuance roughly halves about every four years (the halving), which many participants watch closely.
  • Sentiment and positioning: crypto markets are heavily influenced by retail and leveraged traders, which can cause rapid cascades of forced liquidations.

Trading hours and market structure

Bitcoin trades 24 hours a day, seven days a week, on many exchanges around the world. There is no closing bell, so big moves can happen on a Sunday night.

Spot gold (XAU/USD) trades almost around the clock on weekdays through the global over-the-counter market. Activity moves through Asian, London and New York sessions. It closes at the weekend, and most brokers also have a short daily break. Liquidity and volatility in gold often rise around the London and New York sessions and around major US economic releases, such as inflation and employment data or central bank decisions.

Gold's weekend closure creates a specific risk: the price can open on Monday at a different level from Friday's close, known as a gap. Bitcoin has no gaps of this kind, but it can move a long way while traditional markets are shut.

Is bitcoin digital gold? A correlation that changes over time

Bitcoin is often called digital gold because its supply is limited and it is not controlled by a central bank. In practice, its price has not consistently behaved like gold.

Correlation measures how closely two assets move together. A value near +1 means they tend to move in the same direction, near −1 means opposite directions, and near zero means little consistent relationship. The correlation between gold and bitcoin has historically been low and unstable. In some periods they have risen together; in others they have moved in opposite directions. Over long stretches there has been no clear link at all.

Two points matter for anyone thinking about investing in gold and bitcoin:

  • Correlation is not a constant. A relationship measured over the past year can change quickly, so it should never be treated as a guarantee.
  • Correlations tend to rise in panics. During acute liquidity crunches, such as the market crash of March 2020, investors sold almost everything to raise cash. Gold and bitcoin both fell at the same time, even though they recovered differently afterwards.

What diversification across assets really does (and does not do)

Diversification is one of the most quoted ideas in investing and one of the most misunderstood. It helps to be precise about it.

What it can do

  • Reduce dependence on one market. If all your exposure is to bitcoin, a long crypto bear market affects everything you hold. Adding an asset with different drivers means your results are not tied to a single cycle.
  • Smooth the overall ride. When two assets do not move in lockstep, losses in one may sometimes be partly offset by stability or gains in the other. That can reduce the swings of the combined portfolio.
  • Widen the set of opportunities. For an active strategy, two markets with different behaviour mean more conditions in which there may be something to trade.

What it does not do

  • It does not guarantee a profit or protect against loss. Both assets can fall at once.
  • It does not fix a poor strategy. Spreading a flawed approach across two markets just spreads the flaw.
  • It does not cancel leverage risk. A leveraged position in gold can lose money as fast as a leveraged position in bitcoin if it is too large.
  • It is not automatic. Adding an asset with much higher volatility can make a portfolio riskier, not safer, unless the sizes are balanced.

A simple hypothetical example

Imagine two portfolios of £10,000. These figures are round and purely illustrative.

  • Portfolio A holds £10,000 of bitcoin only.
  • Portfolio B holds £5,000 of bitcoin and £5,000 of gold.

Suppose that over a given period bitcoin falls by 40% and gold rises by 5%. Portfolio A drops to £6,000. In Portfolio B, the bitcoin half falls to £3,000 and the gold half rises to £5,250, giving £8,250 in total.

Now reverse it: bitcoin rises by 40% and gold falls by 5%. Portfolio A grows to £14,000, while Portfolio B ends at £11,750. Diversification reduced the damage in the bad scenario and the gains in the good one. That is the trade-off.

Finally, suppose both fall, bitcoin by 30% and gold by 10%. Portfolio A ends at £7,000 and Portfolio B at £8,000. Diversification softened the loss but did not prevent it.

Why an automated strategy adds a second asset: a real example

Automated strategies face the same issue as individual investors. A system that trades only one market depends entirely on how that market behaves. When conditions suit it, things may go well. When the market turns choppy, illiquid or directionless for months, there may be little the strategy can do.

As a real example, Cortex Automation's algorithm traded only bitcoin (BTC/USD) until the end of October 2026. Since then, as the account history shows, it has also traded gold (XAU/USD). Client funds stay in each client's own broker account throughout. It is too early to say anything about results on gold, and no figures are given here. The reasons for the change illustrate the principles above:

  • Different behaviour. Gold reacts to the dollar, interest rates and geopolitics, while bitcoin reacts mostly to liquidity and crypto-specific sentiment. The two often present opportunities at different times.
  • Different hours and conditions. Gold's activity is concentrated around the London and New York sessions and macroeconomic releases, while bitcoin trades continuously. Together they cover a wider range of market conditions.
  • Not depending on one cycle. Crypto markets have historically gone through long boom-and-bust phases. A second, unrelated market reduces reliance on whichever phase bitcoin happens to be in.
  • Protecting capital. Spreading risk across two assets with separate drivers is one way to limit the impact of a bad period in either one, provided position sizes reflect each asset's volatility.
  • Testing different time frames. Gold allows different approaches to be evaluated, from scalping (very short trades) to intraday (positions closed within the day) and swing trading (positions held for several days). Each carries its own costs and risks.

None of this guarantees better outcomes. A new market brings new risks, such as different spreads, gap risk at the weekend reopening and sensitivity to scheduled economic news. Any strategy needs time and a meaningful sample of trades before its behaviour on a new asset can be judged.

The risks of trading gold and bitcoin

Whether you invest directly or use an automated service, keep these risks in mind:

  • Leverage. Many brokers offer gold and bitcoin as leveraged products such as CFDs. Leverage magnifies both gains and losses, and many retail CFD accounts lose money.
  • Volatility and sudden moves. Bitcoin can move dramatically in hours. Gold can jump on unexpected news or data.
  • Gaps and slippage. Orders may be filled at worse prices than expected, especially at gold's weekly reopening or during fast markets.
  • Costs. Spreads, commissions and overnight financing charges add up, particularly for short-term strategies with many trades.
  • Correlation risk. The relationship between the two assets can change without warning, and in a crisis both may fall together.
  • Strategy risk. Past behaviour of any system, manual or automated, does not guarantee how it will behave in future market conditions.
  • Counterparty and platform risk. Check who holds your money and how the broker or exchange is regulated in your country.

How to approach investing in gold and bitcoin sensibly

  1. Define your goal. Long-term holding, active trading and delegating to an automated strategy are different activities with different risks.
  2. Size by risk, not by amount. Bitcoin's higher volatility usually means a smaller position is needed for comparable risk.
  3. Understand the product. Physical gold, gold ETFs, spot bitcoin and leveraged CFDs behave very differently in terms of costs, ownership and risk.
  4. Only use money you can afford to lose, especially with leveraged or crypto exposure.
  5. Watch the calendar. For gold, know when key US data and central bank meetings are scheduled. For bitcoin, remember the market never closes.
  6. Review regularly. Correlations, volatility and your own circumstances change, so revisit your allocation over time.

Final thoughts

Gold and bitcoin are often mentioned together, but they are very different assets. Gold is a long-established safe haven shaped by rates, the dollar and geopolitics. Bitcoin is a young, highly volatile asset shaped by liquidity and sentiment. Combining them can reduce reliance on any one market and widen the conditions in which a strategy can operate. It cannot eliminate losses, and it does not replace sound risk management.

Frequently asked questions

Is it a good idea to invest in both gold and bitcoin?

It can reduce dependence on a single market because the two assets have different price drivers, but it does not guarantee profits or prevent losses. Whether it suits you depends on your goals, time horizon and tolerance for risk.

Is bitcoin correlated with gold?

Historically, the correlation between gold and bitcoin has been low and unstable. They have sometimes moved together and sometimes in opposite directions, and in sharp market panics both can fall at the same time.

Which is more volatile, gold or bitcoin?

Bitcoin has historically been much more volatile than gold, with large daily swings and deep drawdowns. Gold also moves, but its typical price swings have been considerably smaller.

When can you trade gold and bitcoin?

Bitcoin trades 24/7. Spot gold (XAU/USD) trades almost around the clock on weekdays, with most brokers applying a short daily break, and it closes at the weekend.

Is gold still a safe haven?

Gold is widely regarded as a safe haven and often attracts demand during uncertainty, but it is not risk-free. Its price can fall, particularly when real interest rates or the US dollar rise.

Does diversification remove risk?

No. Diversification can reduce the impact of a bad period in one asset, but it cannot eliminate risk, especially when correlations rise in a crisis or when leverage is used.

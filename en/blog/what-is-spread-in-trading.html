What Is Spread in Trading and What Does It Really Cost?

Updated 2026-11-19 · 6 min read

What is spread in trading? It is the gap between the price at which you can buy an asset and the price at which you can sell it, and you pay it every time you open a position, even with a broker that advertises zero commission. This guide shows how to calculate it, how much it adds up to over a month and how to keep it under control.

What is spread in trading, in plain terms?

Every asset on a trading platform shows two prices. The ask is the price you pay when you buy. The bid is the price you receive when you sell. The ask is always slightly higher than the bid.

The spread is the difference between the two. It is the most common way brokers and market makers get paid for always being ready to take the other side of your trade.

In practice, every position starts a little in the red. If you bought and immediately sold without the market moving at all, you would lose exactly the spread.

How do you calculate the spread? A simple example

Suppose, purely as an illustration, that EUR/USD is quoted like this:

  • Bid: 1.1000
  • Ask: 1.1002

The difference is 0.0002. In currency markets the fourth decimal place is called a pip, so this spread is 2 pips.

To turn pips into money, you need the position size:

  • On a standard lot (100,000 units of the base currency), one EUR/USD pip is worth about 10 dollars.
  • On a mini lot (0.1 lots, or 10,000 units), one pip is worth about 1 dollar.
  • On a micro lot (0.01 lots), one pip is worth about 0.10 dollars.

So a 0.1-lot trade with a 2-pip spread costs roughly 2 dollars the moment you enter. On a single trade that is small. The trouble starts when trades pile up.

The general formula is: spread cost = spread in pips × pip value × number of lots.

What about shares, gold or bitcoin?

The logic is identical; only the unit changes. On a share the spread is measured in cents, on gold in dollars per ounce, on bitcoin in dollars per coin. What matters is how much money each trade costs you.

How much does the spread cost over a month?

You pay the spread on every trade, winners and losers alike, so frequent traders feel it far more. The table below uses the same hypothetical numbers: 0.1 lots, 2 pips, about 2 dollars per trade and 20 trading days.

At ten trades a day, the strategy would need to make 8% a month just to break even. That is why the spread matters so much for styles such as scalping, which aim for gains of only a few pips at a time.

Fixed vs variable spread: what is the difference?

Brokers usually offer one of two models:

  • Fixed spread: stays the same most of the time. It makes costs easy to predict, but it is often a bit wider in normal conditions, and some brokers may still widen it or reject orders during extreme moves.
  • Variable (floating) spread: follows supply and demand in real time. It can be very tight when markets are calm and liquid, and widen sharply when they are not.

Neither is better in every case. It depends on when you trade, what you trade and how often.

When does the spread widen?

A variable spread moves around. It tends to widen in situations like these:

  • Major economic releases, such as interest rate decisions from the European Central Bank or the US Federal Reserve, jobs reports or inflation figures.
  • Low-liquidity hours, for example between the New York close and the Asian open, or when markets reopen on Sunday evening.
  • Thinly traded assets, such as exotic currency pairs, small-cap shares or some cryptocurrencies.
  • High volatility, when market makers protect themselves by charging more.

A spread that is usually 1 or 2 pips can multiply for a few seconds or minutes at those times.

Spread, commission and swap: the other trading costs

The spread is rarely the only cost. When comparing brokers, look at the full picture:

  • Commission: some accounts (often labelled raw or ECN) offer very tight spreads but charge a fixed fee per lot. Others charge no commission and build everything into the spread.
  • Swap or overnight financing: a cost (or occasionally a credit) for holding a leveraged position overnight.
  • Slippage: the gap between the price you asked for and the price you actually got, more common in fast markets.

A commission-free account with a 2-pip spread can end up more expensive than one with a 0.2-pip spread plus commission. You only know by adding everything up.

How can you reduce the impact of the spread?

  1. Compare total cost per trade, not just the headline spread. Average spreads published by brokers are more useful than minimum ones.
  2. Avoid opening positions around big news releases, unless your strategy is built for them.
  3. Favour liquid markets, where spreads tend to be tighter.
  4. Trade less often if your expected gain per trade is small relative to the spread.
  5. Include the spread in your tests: a backtest that ignores costs can look far better than reality.

What are the risks linked to the spread?

  • Unexpected stop-outs: buy positions close at the bid, so a sudden widening of the spread can trigger a stop loss even if the chart never seems to reach that level.
  • Leverage magnifies the cost: with large positions relative to your account, the spread eats a bigger slice of your capital.
  • Costs hidden in frequency: small differences per trade become large over months.
  • Complex products: leveraged CFDs and forex can lead to rapid loss of the money invested.

Understanding the spread does not remove these risks, but it helps you make more informed decisions and keep expectations realistic.

Summary

The spread in trading is the difference between the buy and sell price of an asset. It is a cost paid on every trade, it can be fixed or variable, and it tends to widen around news or in thin markets. On a single trade it looks small, but added up over a month it can decide whether a strategy makes or loses money.

Frequently asked questions

What is spread in trading?

It is the difference between the ask (buy) price and the bid (sell) price of an asset. It acts as a cost you pay each time you open a position.

Is a 1-pip spread good?

On a very liquid pair like EUR/USD, 1 pip is generally considered tight. But add commissions and swaps to know the real cost.

Do you pay the spread on entry and exit?

You pay it once per round trip: you buy at the higher price and sell at the lower one. In practice the position opens with a small loss equal to the spread.

Why does the spread widen during news?

Uncertainty rises and liquidity drops for a short time, so market makers widen the gap between prices to protect themselves.

Fixed or variable spread: which should I choose?

Fixed is more predictable; variable is usually tighter in calm markets but widens during volatile moments. The right choice depends on when, what and how often you trade.

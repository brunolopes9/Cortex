What Is Forex? A Beginner's Guide to the Currency Market

Updated 2026-11-10 · 10 min read

Forex, short for foreign exchange, is the global market where currencies are bought and sold against each other, such as euros for US dollars. Traders aim to profit from changes in exchange rates, usually through a broker and often using leverage, which magnifies both gains and losses. This guide explains how it works, what it costs and the risks you need to understand first.

What is forex? The short answer

If you are asking what is forex, the simplest answer is this: it is the worldwide market for exchanging one currency for another. Every time a company pays a foreign supplier, a tourist changes money at an airport or a central bank adjusts its reserves, currencies change hands. The forex market (also called FX or the currency market) is where those exchange rates are set.

It is the largest financial market in the world. According to the Bank for International Settlements' 2022 triennial survey, average daily turnover was around 7.5 trillion US dollars. Unlike a stock exchange, forex has no single central building or venue. It is an over-the-counter (OTC) market, made up of a network of banks, brokers, funds and other institutions trading electronically.

Most participants are not speculators. Businesses hedge currency exposure, governments manage reserves and investors convert money to buy foreign assets. Retail traders, meaning private individuals, make up a relatively small share of total volume, but they are the focus of this guide.

How forex trading works: currency pairs explained

Currencies are always quoted in pairs, because you can only value one currency in terms of another. In the pair EUR/USD, the euro is the base currency and the US dollar is the quote currency. A price of 1.1000 means one euro costs 1.10 US dollars.

When you trade a pair, you are doing two things at once:

  • Buying (going long) means you expect the base currency to strengthen against the quote currency.
  • Selling (going short) means you expect the base currency to weaken against the quote currency.

Because you can sell as easily as you buy, it is possible to aim for a profit whether a currency rises or falls. Equally, it is possible to lose money in either direction if the market moves against you.

Major, minor and exotic pairs

Currency pairs are usually grouped into three categories:

  • Majors: pairs that include the US dollar and another heavily traded currency, such as EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY, USD/CHF, AUD/USD, USD/CAD and NZD/USD. They tend to be the most liquid and have the lowest trading costs.
  • Minors (crosses): pairs of major currencies that do not include the US dollar, such as EUR/GBP, EUR/JPY or GBP/JPY.
  • Exotics: a major currency paired with the currency of a smaller or emerging economy, such as USD/TRY or EUR/ZAR. These are usually less liquid, more expensive to trade and can move sharply.

What is a pip in forex?

A pip (percentage in point) is the standard unit used to measure price movement. For most pairs, a pip is the fourth decimal place, or 0.0001. If EUR/USD moves from 1.1000 to 1.1001, it has moved one pip. For pairs involving the Japanese yen, a pip is the second decimal place, or 0.01, so USD/JPY moving from 150.00 to 150.01 is one pip.

Many brokers also show a fifth decimal place (or third for yen pairs), known as a pipette or fractional pip, equal to one tenth of a pip.

Lots and pip value

Trade sizes in forex are measured in lots:

  • Standard lot: 100,000 units of the base currency
  • Mini lot: 10,000 units
  • Micro lot: 1,000 units

The value of one pip depends on the trade size and the pair. For EUR/USD, where the dollar is the quote currency, one pip is worth roughly 10 US dollars on a standard lot, 1 dollar on a mini lot and 10 cents on a micro lot.

Hypothetical example: you buy one mini lot (10,000 euros) of EUR/USD at 1.1000. The price rises to 1.1050, a move of 50 pips. At about 1 dollar per pip, the gain is roughly 50 dollars before costs. If the price had instead fallen to 1.0950, you would have lost roughly 50 dollars. The same pip movement produces gains or losses of the same size.

Spreads and other trading costs

Every forex quote has two prices:

  • The bid: the price at which you can sell.
  • The ask (or offer): the price at which you can buy.

The difference between them is the spread, and it is the most basic cost of trading. If EUR/USD is quoted at 1.0998 / 1.1000, the spread is 2 pips. When you open a trade, you immediately start slightly in the red, because you buy at the higher price and could only sell at the lower one.

Spreads tend to be tighter on major pairs and during busy trading hours, and wider on exotic pairs, at quiet times and around major news releases. Other costs to look out for include:

  • Commissions: some account types charge a fixed fee per lot in exchange for tighter spreads.
  • Overnight financing (swap or rollover): if you hold a leveraged position past the daily cut-off, you may pay or receive interest, depending on the interest rate difference between the two currencies and the broker's terms.
  • Slippage: in fast markets, your order may be filled at a worse price than requested.

These costs may look small on a single trade, but they add up, especially for strategies that trade frequently.

Leverage and margin in forex

Leverage is one of the defining features of retail forex, and it is the main reason the market carries high risk. Leverage lets you control a large position with a relatively small deposit, called margin.

With leverage of 30:1, for example, you need margin of about 3.33% of the position's value. To open a position worth 10,000 euros, you would need around 333 euros of margin.

A worked example of leverage

Consider two hypothetical traders, each with an account of 1,000 pounds, trading a pair that moves 1% against them:

ScenarioPosition sizeEffective leverageLoss from a 1% adverse moveShare of account lost
Trader A1,000 pounds1:110 pounds1%
Trader B30,000 pounds30:1300 pounds30%

The same 1% move costs Trader B thirty times as much. Leverage does not change the direction of the market; it only multiplies the outcome, good or bad.

Margin calls and stop-outs

If losses reduce your account equity below the level required to support your open positions, your broker may issue a margin call and, if equity keeps falling, automatically close positions (a stop-out). This can lock in losses at the worst moment. In the UK and EU, retail clients of regulated brokers benefit from negative balance protection, meaning you cannot lose more than the money in your account, but you can still lose all of it.

Forex trading sessions and market hours

Because currencies are traded across the globe, the forex market runs 24 hours a day, five days a week, from Sunday evening to Friday evening (UK time). Activity moves around the world in a series of overlapping sessions:

SessionApproximate hours (UK time)Typical characteristics
Sydney10pm to 7amUsually the quietest; AUD and NZD pairs active
TokyoMidnight to 9amJPY pairs active; often steadier price action
London8am to 5pmTypically the busiest session; high liquidity
New York1pm to 10pmUSD pairs very active; major US data releases

Exact times shift with daylight saving changes. The overlap between London and New York, roughly early afternoon UK time, is usually the most liquid period, with tighter spreads and larger moves. Weekends are closed, which means prices can open on Monday at a different level from Friday's close, a so-called gap.

What moves exchange rates?

Currency prices reflect supply and demand, which are driven by many factors, including:

  • Interest rates and central bank policy: higher rates, or expectations of them, can attract capital to a currency.
  • Economic data: inflation, employment, growth and trade figures.
  • Political events: elections, referendums and government policy changes.
  • Risk sentiment: in periods of stress, money often flows towards currencies seen as safe havens.
  • Unexpected shocks: sudden policy decisions or geopolitical events can move prices sharply within seconds.

Traders typically use fundamental analysis (studying these economic drivers), technical analysis (studying price charts and patterns) or a combination. Neither approach can reliably predict the future.

How retail traders access the forex market

Private individuals do not usually trade directly with the large banks at the heart of the market. Instead, they open an account with a retail broker, which provides a trading platform and access to prices. Common ways to trade include:

  • Spot forex: buying or selling a currency pair for near-immediate settlement, usually rolled over rather than delivered.
  • Contracts for difference (CFDs): you speculate on price changes without owning the currency. Widely available in the UK, EU and Australia, but not to retail clients in the US.
  • Spread betting: a UK and Ireland product where you bet an amount per pip of movement. Profits are currently free of UK capital gains tax for most individuals, though tax treatment depends on personal circumstances and can change.
  • Futures and options: exchange-traded currency contracts, more common among experienced traders.

Forex regulation basics

Because retail forex is leveraged and high risk, many countries regulate the brokers that offer it. Well-known regulators include:

  • United Kingdom: Financial Conduct Authority (FCA)
  • European Union: national regulators such as CySEC (Cyprus) and BaFin (Germany), following rules set by ESMA
  • United States: Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) and the National Futures Association (NFA)
  • Australia: Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC)

In the UK and EU, retail clients face leverage limits of 30:1 on major currency pairs and 20:1 on non-major pairs, along with negative balance protection and mandatory risk warnings. In the US, retail leverage is capped at 50:1 on major pairs and 20:1 on others. Regulated brokers must also typically keep client money segregated from their own funds.

Before opening an account, check the broker's name and licence number directly on the regulator's official register. Be very cautious of firms promising guaranteed or unusually high returns, pressuring you to deposit quickly or asking you to transfer money to personal accounts. Unregulated offshore brokers and forex scams are a well-documented problem.

The risks of forex trading

Forex is accessible, but accessible is not the same as easy. Key risks include:

  • Leverage risk: small price moves can cause large losses relative to your deposit.
  • Volatility and gaps: news events can move prices faster than stop-loss orders can be filled.
  • Cost drag: spreads, commissions and swaps reduce returns, particularly with frequent trading.
  • Counterparty and broker risk: your protections depend heavily on who regulates your broker.
  • Behavioural risk: overtrading, chasing losses and abandoning a plan are common causes of losses.

Regulated brokers in the UK and EU are required to publish the percentage of their retail accounts that lose money on CFDs, and those figures typically show that a majority of retail clients lose. Read that warning on any broker's website before you trade.

Getting started sensibly

If you still want to explore forex, a cautious approach might look like this:

  1. Learn the basics covered here until pips, spreads, lots and margin feel familiar.
  2. Practise on a demo account with virtual money to understand the platform.
  3. Choose a broker regulated by a recognised authority and verify it on the official register.
  4. Use low leverage and small position sizes, such as micro lots.
  5. Decide in advance how much you are prepared to risk per trade and in total, and use stop-loss orders.
  6. Only trade with money you can afford to lose entirely.

Final thoughts

So, what is forex? It is a vast, global, round-the-clock market for exchanging currencies, used by businesses, governments and investors, and accessible to individuals through brokers. Understanding currency pairs, pips, spreads, leverage, margin and trading sessions is the foundation. Understanding the risks, especially how leverage magnifies losses, is just as important.

Frequently asked questions

What is forex in simple terms?

Forex (foreign exchange) is the global market where currencies are exchanged, such as swapping pounds for euros. Traders try to profit from changes in exchange rates between currency pairs.

How much money do I need to start trading forex?

Many brokers allow small deposits and micro lots of 1,000 units, so the entry amount can be low. However, you should only use money you can afford to lose entirely, as leveraged trading is high risk.

Is forex trading legal in the UK?

Yes. Forex trading is legal in the UK, and firms offering it to retail clients must be authorised by the Financial Conduct Authority. You can check a broker on the FCA register.

What is a pip in forex?

A pip is the standard unit of price movement. For most pairs it is 0.0001, and for Japanese yen pairs it is 0.01. On a standard lot of EUR/USD, one pip is worth about 10 US dollars.

Can you lose more than you deposit in forex?

With regulated brokers in the UK and EU, retail clients have negative balance protection, so losses are limited to the account balance. Elsewhere, rules vary, and you could owe more than you deposited.

When is the forex market open?

The forex market is open 24 hours a day, five days a week, from Sunday evening to Friday evening UK time. The London and New York overlap is usually the busiest period.

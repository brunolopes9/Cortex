European stocks fall: what happened and what it means for you

Updated 2026-11-28 · 6 min read

European stocks fall sharply in the week to 20 November 2026, with the STOXX 600 heading for its worst week since March as technology shares sold off on doubts about artificial intelligence. Lisbon's PSI fell too, though less than Frankfurt or Paris, and markets clawed back part of the losses on Monday. Here is what we know and what it means for ordinary investors.

European stocks fall: what happened this week

European stocks fall was the story of the week of 16 to 20 November 2026. According to Reuters, the STOXX 600, an index of around 600 large European companies, was on course on Friday for its worst week since March, dragged down mainly by technology companies.

Portugal's stock market followed the same direction, but with smaller drops than Europe's biggest exchanges. On Monday, 23 November, markets across Europe recovered part of the ground they had lost.

Below we set out the confirmed facts, the likely reasons and what a week like this means if you hold shares or funds.

How much did the Lisbon stock market fall?

According to closing figures reported by Lusa, the Portuguese news agency, the PSI, Lisbon's main index, had two losing sessions at the end of the week and a small gain the following Monday.

On Thursday, 19 November, the PSI slipped 0.94% to 9,410.29 points. On Friday it lost a further 1.39% to 9,279.88, with 13 of its 16 companies in the red. Bank BCP was the biggest faller that day, down 4.05%. On Monday the PSI rose 0.39% to 9,315.86 points.

Why did European stocks fall?

Doubts about the payoff from artificial intelligence

The reason most often cited by international news agencies is growing scepticism about artificial intelligence. Big technology firms have spent enormous sums on data centres and chips in recent years. This week, more investors started to question whether that spending will turn into profits as quickly as hoped.

Reuters reported that European technology was the hardest-hit sector, with chipmakers and chip equipment suppliers falling heavily. When shares that have risen a lot start to slip, some investors also sell to lock in earlier gains, which can make the move bigger.

Why did Lisbon fall less than Frankfurt?

The PSI has very few technology companies. It is made up mostly of banks, energy firms, retailers, paper producers and construction groups. When the pressure comes mainly from tech, Lisbon tends to feel it less than markets where tech stocks carry more weight.

That does not mean Portuguese shares are sheltered. On Friday, banks and energy companies also fell, because on nervous days investors tend to cut risk across the board.

Markets bounced on Monday: is the sell-off over?

On Monday, 23 November, Europe's main markets recovered some of their losses, with technology shares leading the rebound, Reuters reported. Frankfurt gained 2.15% and Lisbon 0.39%, according to Lusa.

Nobody knows whether the recovery will last. One good day after a bad week does not prove the trend has turned, and it does not mean the falls will return either. Markets can swing for weeks until there is more information, for example on company results or interest rates.

As for the ECB (European Central Bank), its next monetary policy meeting is on 16 and 17 December. In October it kept its deposit rate at 2.50%.

What does a stock market fall mean for investors?

If you own shares or funds, a week like this shows up as a lower portfolio value. A simple, hypothetical example makes it clearer.

Say Anna has 10,000 euros in an index fund (ETF) that tracks European shares. If the fund drops 5% in a week, her holding is worth 9,500 euros. That 500-euro loss only becomes permanent if she sells at that point. If she stays invested, the value keeps moving with the market, up or down.

One detail people often miss: recovering from a fall takes a bigger percentage gain than the fall itself. To get from 9,500 back to 10,000 euros, the fund needs to rise about 5.3%, not 5%.

Now suppose Anna invests 200 euros a month. If a fund unit cost 100 euros and now costs 95, the same 200 euros buys about 2.1 units instead of 2. Regular investors buy more cheaply during falls, but that only pays off if the market rises again over time, which is never guaranteed.

What should you do when stock markets fall?

There is no single right answer, but a few questions can help you decide calmly:

  • Will I need this money soon? Money you need in the next few months should not sit in assets that can drop 5% or more in a week.
  • Is my portfolio diversified? Holding everything in one sector, such as tech, makes weeks like this hurt more.
  • Has my plan changed, or just the price? If your goals and time frame are the same, selling on impulse can turn a temporary loss into a real one.
  • Am I using leverage? Products such as CFDs magnify both gains and losses. In a sharp sell-off, losses can be much larger than expected.

What are the risks?

  • Shares can keep falling for weeks or months, and there is no set date for a recovery.
  • Technology companies tend to swing more than the market as a whole.
  • Decisions made under pressure, such as selling everything after a fall, often lock in losses.
  • Leveraged products can lead to fast and large losses.

What Is Drawdown? Maximum Drawdown, Recovery Maths and How to Read It

Updated 2026-11-13 · 9 min read

A drawdown is the fall in the value of an account or investment from its highest point (its peak) to a later low point (its trough), usually shown as a percentage. Maximum drawdown is the largest of those falls over a given period. It tells you how much pain a strategy has actually delivered, which the headline return often hides.

What is drawdown? A simple definition

If you are asking what is drawdown, the short answer is that it measures how far an account has fallen from its previous high. It is not the same as a loss on a single trade. It looks at the whole account, or the whole investment, over time.

Drawdown is usually given as a percentage:

Drawdown = (Peak value − Trough value) ÷ Peak value × 100

The peak is the highest value reached before the fall. The trough is the lowest value reached before a new peak is set. A drawdown period runs until the account climbs back above its old peak. Until then, the account is often described as being underwater.

A worked example

Imagine a hypothetical account that starts at 10,000. It grows to 13,000, then falls to 9,750, and later recovers.

  • Peak: 13,000
  • Trough: 9,750
  • Drawdown: (13,000 − 9,750) ÷ 13,000 = 3,250 ÷ 13,000 = 25%

Notice something important. Measured from the starting deposit of 10,000, the account is down only 2.5%. But measured from its peak, it has lost a quarter of its value. Drawdown is always measured from the peak, because that is the money the investor actually had at that moment.

What is maximum drawdown (MDD)?

An account will usually go through many drawdowns: some small, some large. Maximum drawdown, often shortened to MDD or max DD, is the largest peak-to-trough fall recorded over the period you are looking at.

Maximum drawdown answers a blunt question: what is the worst fall someone would have experienced if they had invested at the worst possible moment and held on?

Two points are worth remembering:

  • It is historical. Maximum drawdown only describes what has already happened. A future drawdown can be larger, and frequently is, especially when a track record is short.
  • It depends on the period. A five-year maximum drawdown that includes a market crash will look very different from a one-year figure taken during a calm market.

Drawdown duration and recovery time

Depth is only half the story. Investors also look at:

  • Drawdown duration: how long it took to go from peak to trough.
  • Recovery time: how long it took to climb from the trough back to the old peak.
  • Time underwater: the total time from the old peak to a new high.

A 15% drawdown recovered in two months feels very different from a 15% drawdown that leaves the account below its peak for three years. Both have the same depth, but the second is far harder to live with.

The recovery maths: why losses are harder to win back

This is the part of drawdown that surprises most people. Because gains and losses are percentages of a changing base, a loss requires a larger percentage gain to recover.

If an account of 10,000 falls 50% to 5,000, a 50% gain only takes it to 7,500. It needs a 100% gain to get back to 10,000.

The formula is:

Gain needed to recover = Drawdown ÷ (1 − Drawdown)

10%11.1%
20%25%
25%33.3%

Small drawdowns are relatively easy to recover from. Large ones become very hard, and beyond around 50% the required gain rises steeply. This asymmetry is the main reason experienced traders focus on limiting drawdown rather than chasing the biggest possible return.

In the earlier example, the account that fell 25% from 13,000 to 9,750 needs a gain of about 33.3% just to get back to where it was.

How to read drawdown on a track record

Whether you are looking at a fund factsheet, a trading signal provider or an automated strategy, drawdown figures can be presented in ways that look better than reality. Here is what to check.

1. Balance drawdown vs equity drawdown

In trading, balance usually reflects only closed trades, while equity includes open positions at their current market value. A strategy that holds losing trades open for a long time can show a smooth balance curve while its equity falls sharply. Equity drawdown is the more honest measure, because that is what the account would be worth if everything were closed at that moment.

2. Frequency of the data

Drawdown calculated from monthly figures can miss deep falls that happened and recovered within the month. Daily, or ideally intraday, equity data shows the true depth more accurately. If only monthly returns are shown, assume the real intra-month drawdown may have been larger.

3. Length and type of the record

  • A short track record may simply not have met a difficult market yet.
  • A backtest (a simulation on historical data) is not the same as live trading. Backtests can be over-fitted and usually ignore some real costs such as slippage.
  • Look for whether the record covers different market conditions: trending, ranging, calm and volatile periods.

4. Relative or absolute figures

Some reports show drawdown as a percentage of the peak; others show it in currency or relative to the initial deposit. Percentage from peak is the standard and the most comparable. Make sure you know which one you are reading.

5. Recovery behaviour

Look at what happened after the largest drawdowns. Did the strategy recover steadily, or did it increase position sizes to win losses back quickly? Rising risk after losses (as in martingale-style approaches) can produce a smooth curve for a long time, followed by a very large drawdown.

Drawdown vs volatility: what is the difference?

Drawdown and volatility are both risk measures, but they describe different things.

Volatility, usually measured as the standard deviation of returns, shows how widely returns swing around their average. It treats upward and downward moves the same way, and it does not care about the order in which returns happen.

Drawdown only looks at falls, and it is path-dependent: the sequence of returns matters. A run of consecutive losing months produces a deep drawdown, while the same losing months spread out between winning months may produce only shallow ones.

A simple illustration

Consider two hypothetical strategies, each with six monthly returns made up of three months at +4% and three months at −4%. Their volatility is identical, because the set of returns is the same.

  • Strategy A alternates: +4%, −4%, +4%, −4%, +4%, −4%. Its maximum drawdown is only 4%.
  • Strategy B has the losses together: +4%, +4%, +4%, −4%, −4%, −4%. Its maximum drawdown is about 11.5%.

Same volatility, very different experience for the investor. That is why volatility alone can understate the real risk of a strategy, and why both measures are worth reading together.

Why drawdown matters more than the headline return

Headline returns are what most marketing puts first. Drawdown is often what decides whether an investor actually gets those returns. There are several reasons.

Compounding works against large losses

As the recovery table shows, deep losses need disproportionately large gains to repair. A strategy that earns strong returns but suffers occasional 50% drawdowns can end up behind a steadier strategy with lower returns and smaller falls.

Behaviour: most people do not hold through deep drawdowns

A return figure assumes the investor stayed invested the whole time. In practice, many people stop a strategy, withdraw money or change approach in the middle of a drawdown, which locks in the loss and misses the recovery. Knowing the historical maximum drawdown in advance helps you judge honestly whether you could tolerate it.

Leverage magnifies drawdown

In leveraged products such as forex or CFDs, a drawdown can trigger margin calls or automatic closure of positions before the account has a chance to recover. A strategy with a deep drawdown history combined with high leverage carries a real risk of losing a large part, or all, of the account.

Return relative to risk is more useful than return alone

One common way to put the two together is the Calmar ratio: annualised return divided by maximum drawdown. As a hypothetical example, a strategy returning 12% a year with a 10% maximum drawdown has a ratio of 1.2. One returning 20% with a 40% maximum drawdown has a ratio of 0.5. The second has the bigger headline number but delivered much less return per unit of pain. Ratios like this are only as good as the data behind them, and they are still backward-looking.

How investors manage drawdown risk

Drawdowns cannot be eliminated, since every strategy that takes risk will experience them. But they can be managed. Common approaches include:

  • Position sizing: risking a small, fixed percentage of the account per trade so that a losing streak does not cause a catastrophic fall.
  • Moderate leverage: lower leverage means each market move has a smaller effect on equity.
  • Stop-losses and risk limits: predefined exit points and daily or weekly loss limits.
  • Diversification: spreading risk across assets or strategies that do not all fall at the same time, though correlations can rise sharply in a crisis.
  • Setting a personal limit: deciding in advance the drawdown you are willing to accept, and what you will do if it is reached.
  • Keeping control of your funds: with some automated services, such as Cortex Automation, the money stays in the client's own broker account, so the client can see equity and drawdown directly rather than relying only on reported figures.

Key points to remember

  • Drawdown is the percentage fall from a peak to a subsequent trough.
  • Maximum drawdown is the largest such fall over a period, and future drawdowns can exceed it.
  • Losses require larger percentage gains to recover: a 50% drawdown needs a 100% gain.
  • Check equity rather than balance, data frequency, record length and whether results are live or simulated.
  • Volatility and drawdown measure different things; read them together.
  • Judge a return by the drawdown taken to achieve it, and by whether you could realistically hold through it.

Frequently asked questions

What is drawdown in simple terms?

Drawdown is how much an account or investment has fallen from its highest value to a later low, usually shown as a percentage. For example, falling from 10,000 to 8,000 is a 20% drawdown.

What is the difference between drawdown and maximum drawdown?

Drawdown refers to any peak-to-trough fall. Maximum drawdown is the largest of those falls over a specific period, showing the worst decline the account experienced.

How much gain do I need to recover from a drawdown?

Use the formula drawdown ÷ (1 − drawdown). A 10% drawdown needs about 11.1% to recover, a 25% drawdown needs 33.3%, and a 50% drawdown needs 100%.

What is a good maximum drawdown?

There is no universal figure. An acceptable drawdown depends on the strategy, the leverage used, the expected return and your own tolerance for losses. Compare drawdown with return and remember future drawdowns can be larger.

Is drawdown the same as volatility?

No. Volatility measures how widely returns vary in both directions, while drawdown measures only falls from a peak and depends on the order of returns. Two strategies can have the same volatility but very different drawdowns.

Should I look at balance or equity drawdown?

Equity drawdown is more informative because it includes open positions at their current value. Balance drawdown only reflects closed trades and can hide losses held in open positions.

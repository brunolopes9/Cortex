How to Diversify Investments: A Step-by-Step Guide to Building a Balanced Portfolio

Updated 2026-11-10 · 10 min read

Learning how to diversify investments means spreading your money across different assets, regions and strategies so that no single loss can sink your whole portfolio. This guide explains what diversification really is, how correlation works, which asset classes to consider and how to build a diversified portfolio step by step, without hype and with the risks spelt out.

If you have ever wondered how to diversify investments properly, you are asking one of the most important questions in personal finance. Diversification is not about owning lots of things for the sake of it. It is about owning things that behave differently from one another, so that when one part of your portfolio struggles, another part may hold up or even rise.

This guide covers the core ideas, the main asset classes, how correlation works, a practical step-by-step method, the mistakes people commonly make and where alternative approaches, such as algorithmic trading, might fit as a small slice of a wider plan.

What is diversification in investing?

Diversification is the practice of spreading your capital across a range of investments to reduce the impact of any single one performing badly. The idea is often summed up as "don't put all your eggs in one basket".

A portfolio holding shares in one company depends entirely on that company. A portfolio holding hundreds of companies across many industries and countries, plus some bonds and cash, depends on no single business, sector or economy.

Diversification works at several levels:

  • Across asset classes – for example shares, bonds, property and cash.
  • Within asset classes – many companies rather than a few, or bonds from different issuers and maturities.
  • Across geographies – not relying solely on your home market.
  • Across sectors – technology, healthcare, energy, consumer goods and so on.
  • Across time – investing gradually rather than all at once.
  • Across strategies – for example passive index investing alongside a small allocation to a different, rules-based approach.

Why diversification matters

To understand why diversification helps, it is useful to separate two kinds of risk.

Specific (unsystematic) risk

This is the risk tied to a single company or sector: a product recall, a fraud scandal, a failed drug trial or a regulatory change. Specific risk can be dramatically reduced by holding many different investments, because the bad news affecting one is unlikely to hit all the others at the same time.

Market (systematic) risk

This is the risk that affects whole markets: recessions, interest rate changes, inflation shocks or global crises. Diversification within shares alone cannot remove market risk, because most shares tend to fall together in a broad sell-off. Spreading across asset classes that respond differently to economic conditions can soften it, but never eliminate it entirely.

The key takeaway: diversification is a way of managing risk, not avoiding it. It aims to make the ride smoother and reduce the chance of a catastrophic, permanent loss, rather than to maximise returns in any given year.

Understanding correlation: the heart of diversification

Correlation measures how closely two investments move together. It ranges from +1 to −1:

  • +1 (perfect positive correlation) – the two always move in the same direction. Owning both adds little diversification.
  • 0 (no correlation) – their movements are unrelated.
  • −1 (perfect negative correlation) – they always move in opposite directions.

In practice, perfect correlations are rare. What you are looking for are assets with low or moderately negative correlations to each other. Combining them can reduce overall portfolio volatility without necessarily reducing long-term expected returns in proportion.

A simple hypothetical example

Imagine two investments, A and B. In a given year, A rises 10% while B falls 2%. The following year, A falls 5% while B rises 6%. Each one on its own is bumpy, but a 50/50 mix would have moved much less: roughly +4% in the first year and +0.5% in the second. These numbers are purely illustrative, but they show the mechanism.

Correlations are not fixed

A crucial point that many guides skip: correlations change over time. During severe market stress, assets that usually move independently can suddenly fall together as investors rush for cash. Diversification still helps in a crisis, but often less than calm-period data would suggest. Build your portfolio expecting this, not hoping it will not happen.

How to diversify investments step by step

Here is a practical framework you can adapt to your own circumstances.

  1. Clarify your goals and time horizon. Money needed in two years should be treated very differently from money for retirement in 25 years. Longer horizons can generally tolerate more volatility.
  2. Build an emergency fund first. Keep several months of essential expenses in accessible cash. This stops you being forced to sell investments at a bad time.
  3. Assess your risk tolerance honestly. Ask yourself how you would feel, and act, if your portfolio fell 20% or 30% in a year. Your allocation should be one you can stick with.
  4. Choose your core asset allocation. Decide the broad split between shares, bonds and cash. This decision usually has a far bigger effect on your results than which individual funds you pick.
  5. Diversify within each asset class. Broad, low-cost index funds or ETFs can give exposure to hundreds or thousands of companies or bonds in a single holding.
  6. Spread across regions. Many investors overweight their home country. A global equity fund reduces dependence on any one economy or currency.
  7. Consider a small satellite allocation. Once the core is in place, some investors add a modest slice for property, commodities or alternative strategies. Keep it small and understand exactly what it does.
  8. Watch costs and tax. Fees compound over time. Use tax-efficient wrappers where available in your country, such as ISAs and pensions in the UK.
  9. Invest regularly. Contributing a fixed amount monthly (sometimes called pound-cost averaging) spreads your entry points and reduces the risk of investing everything just before a fall.
  10. Review and rebalance. Check your allocation periodically and bring it back to target when it drifts.

Rebalancing: keeping your diversification on track

Over time, assets grow at different rates, so your portfolio drifts away from its targets. If shares have a strong run, a 55% equity allocation might become 65%, leaving you with more risk than you intended.

Rebalancing means selling some of what has grown and buying what has lagged, or directing new contributions to underweight areas. Common approaches include:

  • Calendar rebalancing – for example once or twice a year.
  • Threshold rebalancing – when any allocation drifts by more than a set amount, such as 5 percentage points.
  • Cash-flow rebalancing – using new money to top up underweight assets, which can reduce trading costs and tax.

Rebalancing imposes discipline: it encourages trimming what has become expensive and adding to what has become cheaper, rather than chasing recent winners.

Common diversification mistakes to avoid

Owning many funds that hold the same things

Five different funds tracking large US technology companies are not five different bets. Check the underlying holdings for overlap.

Home bias

Putting most of your money into your own country's market feels familiar, but it concentrates your risk in one economy, often the same one your job and home depend on.

Over-diversification

Holding dozens of products can raise costs and make the portfolio hard to monitor, without meaningfully reducing risk. Beyond a point, more holdings simply add complexity.

Concentration in your employer's shares

Your income already depends on your employer. Holding a large chunk of its shares doubles that exposure.

Chasing performance

Moving money into whatever did best last year often means buying high. Diversification is a long-term discipline, not a rotation strategy.

Ignoring costs

High fees on several products can quietly eat into returns year after year.

Treating diversification as a guarantee

A diversified portfolio can and does lose money, sometimes significantly. It reduces certain risks; it does not remove them.

Where alternative strategies can fit

Beyond traditional assets, some investors look at alternative strategies to add a different source of returns. Examples include market-neutral funds, managed futures and algorithmic (automated) trading, where trades are executed according to pre-defined rules rather than discretionary decisions.

The appeal is that some of these strategies may respond to different drivers from a buy-and-hold equity portfolio, which could, in some conditions, lower overall correlation. However, there are important caveats:

  • They can lose money. Leveraged trading in particular can produce rapid, large losses.
  • Historical results can mislead. Backtests and past track records do not guarantee future performance, and strategies that worked in one market environment may fail in another.
  • Complexity and transparency vary. You should understand how the strategy works, what it trades, how losses are controlled and what you pay.
  • Custody matters. Know where your money is held and who can access it.

For these reasons, alternative strategies are usually treated as a small satellite around a diversified core, sized so that even a total loss of that slice would not derail your financial plans. The limits and risks of diversification

To keep expectations realistic:

  • Diversification cannot protect against broad market declines; it can only soften them.
  • A diversified portfolio will almost never match the single best-performing asset in a given year. That is the trade-off for lower concentration risk.
  • Correlations can rise sharply in a crisis.
  • Currency movements can add or subtract from international returns.
  • Inflation can erode the real value of cash and bonds over long periods.

Understanding these limits helps you stay invested through difficult periods rather than abandoning your plan at the worst moment.

Summary: how to diversify investments sensibly

Start with clear goals and an emergency fund. Choose a core mix of shares, bonds and cash that matches your time horizon and your genuine tolerance for losses. Diversify within and across asset classes and regions, keep costs low, invest regularly and rebalance periodically. If you add alternative strategies, keep them small and make sure you fully understand them.

Frequently asked questions

What is the simplest way to diversify investments?

For many people, a low-cost global equity index fund combined with a bond fund and some cash provides broad diversification across thousands of companies, regions and asset types in just a few holdings.

How many stocks do I need to be diversified?

There is no exact number, but owning only a handful of shares leaves you exposed to company-specific risk. Broad index funds give exposure to hundreds or thousands of companies, which reduces that risk far more than picking individual shares.

Does diversification guarantee I won't lose money?

No. Diversification reduces the impact of any single investment failing, but it cannot remove market-wide risk. A diversified portfolio can still fall in value, sometimes significantly.

How often should I rebalance a diversified portfolio?

Common approaches are once or twice a year, or whenever an allocation drifts by a set threshold such as 5 percentage points from its target. Using new contributions to top up underweight assets is another low-cost option.

Can algorithmic trading be part of a diversified portfolio?

It can be used as a small satellite allocation alongside a diversified core, but it carries its own risks, including potentially large losses. Size it so that losing that slice would not affect your wider plans, and understand the strategy, costs and custody arrangements first.

Is it possible to over-diversify?

Yes. Holding many overlapping funds or products can raise costs and complexity without meaningfully reducing risk. A small number of broad, low-cost funds often achieves better diversification than dozens of similar ones.

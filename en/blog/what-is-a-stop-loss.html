What Is a Stop Loss? A Beginner's Guide to Placing and Using Stops

Updated 2026-11-14 · 10 min read

A stop loss is an order you leave with your broker to close a trade automatically if the price moves against you to a level you chose in advance. It caps how much you can lose on one position, though not always at the exact price. This guide covers how stop-loss orders work, where to place them, how to size a trade from the stop, and the mistakes beginners make most often.

What is a stop loss?

A stop loss (or stop-loss order) is an instruction to your broker to close a position once the market reaches a price you set. If you buy, the stop sits below your entry. If you sell short, it sits above. It is how you decide your maximum planned loss on a trade before you enter, not while you are watching the price move against you.

Beginners searching for what is a stop loss usually want to know two things: does it protect me, and where should I put it? It does protect you, with limits we cover below. Where to put it depends on the market, not on a favourite number.

Here is the basic idea. Without a stop, one bad trade can wipe out weeks or months of gains. With a stop, every trade has a known worst case under normal market conditions, and that makes risk something you can measure and plan for.

How a stop-loss order works at the broker

When you place a stop loss, it is stored on the broker's server, so it keeps working when your computer is switched off. The broker monitors the price, and once your stop level is reached the order is triggered.

What happens next depends on the order type:

  • Stop-market order (standard stop): once triggered, it becomes a market order and fills at the next available price. Execution is almost certain, but the price is not.
  • Stop-limit order: once triggered, it becomes a limit order that will only fill at your limit price or better. You control the price, but if the market moves quickly past your limit, the order may not fill and you stay in the trade.
  • Guaranteed stop-loss order (GSLO): offered by some brokers, usually for a fee or a wider spread. It closes the trade at exactly your price, even through gaps. Availability and terms vary by broker and instrument.

For long positions, the trigger is normally the bid price. For short positions, it is normally the ask. This matters in markets with wide spreads: a short position's stop can be hit when the spread widens, even if the chart (which often shows the bid) never seems to reach your level.

Where to place a stop loss

The most common beginner mistake is choosing a stop because it feels right, such as 20 pips, 5% or £50. The market doesn't care about those numbers. A good stop sits at the price where your trade idea is shown to be wrong, with enough room for normal price noise.

Two widely used approaches are market structure and volatility. Many traders combine them.

1. Placing the stop on market structure

Market structure means the swing highs and lows, support and resistance, and ranges the price has already formed. The logic is simple:

  • If you buy because the price is bouncing off support, the idea is wrong if price breaks clearly below that support. Place the stop a little below it, not exactly on it.
  • If you sell short after a lower high in a downtrend, the idea is wrong if price climbs above that high. Place the stop a little above it.
  • If you trade a breakout from a range, a return deep inside the range usually cancels the setup.

Leave a small buffer past the level. Round numbers and obvious swing points tend to attract many orders, and price often pokes briefly beyond them before turning back.

2. Placing the stop using volatility (ATR)

The Average True Range (ATR) is a standard indicator that measures how far an instrument typically moves over a set number of periods. Fourteen is the usual default. Volatility-based stops scale with the market: wide when it is moving a lot, tighter when it is quiet.

A common method is to place the stop at a multiple of ATR from entry, for example 1.5× or 2× ATR. There is no magic multiple. It depends on your timeframe and strategy, and should be tested, not guessed.

Hypothetical example: a share trades at 50.00 and its daily ATR is 1.50. A 2× ATR stop would sit 3.00 below entry, at 47.00. If the most recent swing low is at 47.40, a stop at 47.00 is both beyond structure and outside normal daily noise. When structure and volatility point to a similar area, the stop is usually on solid ground.

Why a fixed number of pips doesn't work

A 20-pip stop might be generous on a quiet currency pair during the Asian session and far too tight on a volatile pair around a central bank announcement. A fixed distance ignores context. In one market it gets hit by random noise, and in another it gives the trade more room than the idea justifies.

Position sizing from the stop distance

This is the part many beginners skip, and it is the most important. The stop decides where you exit. Position size decides how much that exit costs you. You first set the stop where the market says it should go, then adjust the size of the trade so that a stop-out costs only the amount you planned to risk.

The formula is:

Position size = Amount at risk ÷ (Stop distance × Value per unit of movement)

Worked example: risking 1% of a 10,000 account

All figures are round and hypothetical.

  1. Account size: 10,000.
  2. Risk per trade: 1%, so 100 is the most you plan to lose if the stop is hit.
  3. Entry: buy a share at 50.00.
  4. Stop: 47.00, based on structure and 2× ATR as above. Stop distance = 3.00 per share.
  5. Position size: 100 ÷ 3.00 = 33.3, rounded down to 33 shares.

If the stop is hit, the loss is about 33 × 3.00 = 99, plus costs and any slippage. The position is worth about 1,650, but the planned risk is only around 100.

The same logic applies in forex. Suppose a USD-denominated account of 10,000 risks 100 on EUR/USD with a 50-pip stop. On a standard lot (100,000 units) of EUR/USD, one pip is worth roughly $10. So 100 ÷ (50 × 10) = 0.2 lots. If the stop were 25 pips instead, the size would be 0.4 lots. The risk in money stays the same, and only the size changes.

This is why a wide stop is not automatically riskier than a tight one. What matters is the money at risk, and you control that with position size.

Trailing stops and moving to break-even

Once a trade moves in your favour, you can manage the stop so it protects more of the position.

Trailing stop

A trailing stop follows the price at a set distance as the trade moves in your favour, but never moves back. If you buy at 50.00 with a 3.00 trailing stop and the price rises to 56.00, the stop rises to 53.00. If the price then falls, the stop stays at 53.00.

Trails can use a fixed distance, a multiple of ATR, or recent swing lows and highs. They let a trend run while locking in part of the gain. The downside is that a trail set too tight will close good trades on ordinary pullbacks.

Moving the stop to break-even

Moving to break-even means shifting the stop to your entry price once the trade has gained a certain amount, so a reversal no longer costs you money (apart from costs and possible slippage). It feels safe, but doing it too early often turns trades that would have worked into scratch trades. A common compromise is to wait until price has moved at least the original stop distance in your favour, or has formed a new swing point, before moving to break-even.

Slippage and gaps: the limits of a stop loss

A standard stop loss is not a guarantee of the exit price. Two things can make your loss larger than planned:

  • Slippage: in fast markets, such as around major economic data, there may be no buyers or sellers at your exact stop price. Your stop-market order fills at the next available price, which can be worse.
  • Gaps: markets can open well away from the previous close, for example after a weekend, earnings results or major news. If a share closes at 48.00 and opens at 44.00, a stop at 47.00 will typically fill near 44.00, not 47.00.

Ways to reduce this risk include smaller position sizes, avoiding holding through scheduled high-impact events, being careful with positions over weekends, and using guaranteed stops where they are offered and the cost is justified. None of these removes risk entirely.

Classic stop-loss mistakes to avoid

Moving the stop further away

The price approaches your stop, you feel sure it will turn, and you move the stop lower. This is the most damaging habit in trading. It turns a planned, small loss into an unplanned, larger one, and it breaks your position-sizing maths: the 1% you planned to risk quietly becomes 2%, 3% or more.

Removing the stop entirely

Trading without a stop, or cancelling it mid-trade, leaves your loss open-ended. A trader might get away with it many times, which only makes the one time it fails more painful. If you use leverage, an unprotected position can lose more than you expected very quickly.

Stops that are too tight

Placing the stop inside normal market noise to make a bigger position possible is a common trap. The trade gets stopped out, then moves in the expected direction without you. Many small losses from stops that are too tight can add up to the same damage as one large loss.

Other frequent errors

  • Choosing the stop after choosing the position size, rather than the other way round.
  • Placing stops exactly on obvious round numbers or swing points with no buffer.
  • Ignoring the spread, especially on short positions or illiquid instruments.
  • Forgetting that a stop doesn't protect against gaps.

Key takeaways

  • A stop loss closes your trade automatically at a level you set in advance, which limits your planned loss.
  • Place it where your trade idea is proven wrong, using market structure and volatility, not a random number of pips.
  • Set the stop first, then work out the position size so a stop-out costs a fixed share of your account, such as 1%.
  • Trailing stops and break-even moves can protect gains but cut trades short if used too aggressively.
  • Slippage and gaps mean a standard stop doesn't guarantee your exit price.
  • Never move a stop further away or remove it to avoid taking a loss.

Frequently asked questions

What is a stop loss in simple terms?

It is an order with your broker that automatically closes a trade if the price moves against you to a level you set in advance. It limits your planned loss on that position.

Does a stop loss guarantee my exit price?

No. A standard stop becomes a market order once triggered and can fill at a worse price because of slippage or gaps. Only a guaranteed stop-loss order, offered by some brokers for a fee, fixes the exit price.

How far away should I put my stop loss?

Put it where your trade idea is proven wrong, usually beyond a relevant swing high or low, and outside normal volatility (often measured with ATR). Then adjust your position size so that being stopped out costs only your planned risk.

What is the 1% rule with a stop loss?

It means risking no more than 1% of your account on a single trade. On a 10,000 account that is 100, so you size the position so that the distance from entry to stop costs about 100.

What is the difference between a stop loss and a trailing stop?

A normal stop loss stays at a fixed price. A trailing stop moves with the price as the trade goes in your favour, at a set distance, but never moves back, so it can lock in part of a gain.

Should I ever move my stop loss?

Moving it in the direction of the trade to reduce risk or protect profit, as with a trailing stop or a move to break-even, is common practice. Moving it further away to avoid taking a loss is one of the most damaging trading mistakes.

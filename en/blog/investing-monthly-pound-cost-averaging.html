Investing Monthly: How to Invest a Fixed Amount Every Month

Updated 2026-11-13 · 9 min read

Investing monthly means putting a fixed sum into the markets on a regular schedule, whatever prices are doing, a method known as pound-cost averaging. It suits people who invest from their salary, removes the pressure of timing the market and is easy to automate. It does not remove risk, however, and it will not always beat investing a lump sum.

Investing monthly is one of the simplest and most widely used ways to build long-term savings. Instead of trying to pick the perfect moment, you commit a fixed amount, say £100, £250 or £500, and invest it on the same day each month. Over time you buy into the market at many different prices, which smooths out the effect of short-term ups and downs.

This guide explains how regular investing works, how it compares with investing a lump sum, how to decide how much to put away, how to automate the process and what you can realistically expect.

What is investing monthly (pound-cost averaging)?

When you invest the same amount at regular intervals, you automatically buy more units of an investment when prices are low and fewer when prices are high. In the UK this is usually called pound-cost averaging; in the US the term is dollar-cost averaging. The idea is identical.

The key point is that your contribution stays fixed while the number of units you receive varies. This is different from buying a fixed number of shares each month, where the amount you spend would rise and fall with the price.

A simple worked example

Imagine you invest £200 a month into a fund for four months. The prices below are hypothetical and chosen to keep the maths simple.

You spent £800 and own 105 units, so your average cost per unit is about £7.62, which is lower than the simple average price of £8.25. That gap exists because your fixed £200 bought more units when the price dipped. At the end, with the price back at £10, your 105 units are worth £1,050.

If you had invested the full £800 in month one at £10, you would own 80 units worth £800. In this particular scenario, investing monthly came out ahead because the price fell and then recovered.

Investing monthly vs lump sum: which is better?

This is one of the most common questions, and the honest answer is: it depends on what markets do after you invest, which nobody can know in advance.

When a lump sum tends to win

Take the same £800, but this time prices rise steadily: £10, £11, £12 and £13. Investing £200 a month buys roughly 20, 18.2, 16.7 and 15.4 units, about 70.2 units in total, worth around £913 at £13. Investing the lump sum at £10 buys 80 units, worth £1,040.

Because broad stock markets have tended to rise over long periods, money invested earlier has more time in the market. For that reason, investing a lump sum straight away has historically come out ahead more often than spreading it out. That is a tendency, not a rule.

When investing monthly tends to win

Regular investing tends to do better when markets fall soon after you start, or move sideways with a lot of volatility. It also protects you from the worst-case outcome of putting everything in just before a big drop.

The practical reality

For most people the debate is partly theoretical. If you earn a monthly salary, you do not have a lump sum to invest; investing monthly is simply investing as soon as you can. Where the choice matters is when you receive a large sum at once. Some people choose a middle path, such as investing half immediately and drip-feeding the rest over six to twelve months, accepting a likely small cost in return for peace of mind.

The main advantages of regular investing

  • No need to time the market. Predicting short-term moves is extremely difficult, even for professionals. A fixed schedule removes that decision.
  • Builds a habit. Treating investing like a bill you pay yourself makes it more likely you keep going.
  • Reduces emotional decisions. Falling prices become an opportunity to buy more units rather than a reason to panic.
  • Accessible. Many platforms accept monthly contributions from small amounts, so you do not need a large sum to start.
  • Smooths the entry price. Your average cost reflects many market conditions rather than a single day.

The disadvantages and risks

  • It does not prevent losses. If an investment falls and never recovers, buying it every month means losing money every month. Pound-cost averaging spreads timing risk; it does not remove market risk.
  • Cash waiting to be invested can lag. If you hold a lump sum and drip-feed it, the uninvested portion misses out when markets rise.
  • Costs can add up. A flat dealing fee on each small purchase can eat into returns. A £5 fee on a £50 contribution is a 10% cost before the investment has done anything.
  • False sense of security. Automating contributions is not the same as choosing suitable investments. What you buy still matters most.
  • Inflation and fees erode returns. Ongoing charges and inflation reduce what your money is really worth over time.

How much should you invest each month?

There is no single right figure. The amount should come from your own budget, not from what feels exciting. A sensible order of priorities usually looks like this:

  1. Cover essentials and clear expensive debt. Paying off a credit card charging high interest is usually a better use of money than investing.
  2. Build an emergency fund. Many people aim for three to six months of essential spending in easily accessible cash, so they are not forced to sell investments at a bad time.
  3. Use any workplace pension match. If your employer adds money when you contribute, that is often the first place to invest.
  4. Decide a comfortable monthly figure. Some people use a percentage of take-home pay, such as 10% or 15%, and increase it when their income rises.

Only invest money you can leave untouched for at least five years, ideally longer. Short time frames give markets less opportunity to recover from falls.

What regular contributions can add up to

Suppose you invest £200 a month for 20 years. You would contribute £48,000. If, hypothetically, your investments grew at a steady 5% a year after fees, the pot could reach roughly £82,000. If returns were 0%, you would have just your £48,000; if markets performed poorly, you could have less.

In reality, returns are never steady. Some years are strongly positive, others negative. The example simply shows how compounding works over time; it is not a forecast.

How to automate investing monthly

Automation is what makes regular investing stick. Once set up, it runs without needing willpower each month.

Step 1: Choose an account

Pick an investment platform or broker and, where available, a tax-efficient wrapper. In the UK, that might be a Stocks and Shares ISA or a pension such as a SIPP; other countries have their own equivalents, such as IRAs and 401(k)s in the US. Rules and allowances vary, so check what applies to you.

Step 2: Pick what to invest in

Many regular investors choose diversified, low-cost funds such as global index funds or ETFs, which spread money across many companies. Buying individual shares each month concentrates risk in a handful of businesses.

Step 3: Set up a regular investment plan

Most platforms let you create a direct debit that pulls a fixed amount on a chosen date and buys your selected funds automatically. Some offer regular investing with reduced or no dealing fees, which helps with small amounts.

Step 4: Time it with your payday

Schedule the contribution for a day or two after you are paid, so the money is invested before it can be spent.

Step 5: Review, but not too often

Check once or twice a year that your contributions, investments and risk level still suit your goals. Looking every day tends to encourage reactive decisions.

Realistic expectations for monthly investors

  • Expect falls. Over a long investing life you will almost certainly see periods when your portfolio is worth less than you paid in. This is normal for investments that can grow.
  • Expect it to feel slow at first. In the early years most of your balance is your own contributions. Compounding becomes more noticeable only over longer periods.
  • Expect no guarantees. Past returns of any market or fund do not guarantee future results.
  • Keep going in downturns. Stopping contributions when prices fall removes the main benefit of pound-cost averaging: buying more units when they are cheaper.
  • Watch costs. Over decades, the difference between low and high ongoing charges can be substantial.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Investing money you may need within a year or two.
  • Pausing contributions every time the news turns negative.
  • Paying high dealing fees on small monthly purchases.
  • Putting all monthly contributions into one share or a narrow theme.
  • Never increasing contributions as income grows.
  • Confusing an automated schedule with a guaranteed outcome.

Is investing monthly right for you?

Investing monthly is well suited to people with a regular income, a long time horizon and a wish to avoid trying to time the market. It is less clearly the best choice if you already hold a large sum and are comfortable with the risk of investing it at once, because historically lump sums have more often done better. Either way, the decisions that matter most are how much you can afford to invest, what you invest in, what you pay in fees and whether you stay the course.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to invest monthly or all at once?

Historically, investing a lump sum straight away has more often come out ahead because markets have tended to rise over time. Investing monthly reduces the risk of investing everything just before a fall and suits people investing from their salary.

What is pound-cost averaging?

It is investing a fixed amount at regular intervals, so you buy more units when prices are low and fewer when prices are high. In the US it is called dollar-cost averaging.

How much should I invest each month?

Only what you can afford after covering essentials, clearing expensive debt and building an emergency fund. Many people start with a percentage of take-home pay, such as 10%, and raise it over time.

Can you lose money investing monthly?

Yes. Pound-cost averaging spreads timing risk but does not remove market risk. If your investments fall in value, your portfolio can be worth less than you contributed.

What is the best day of the month to invest?

There is no reliably best day. Most people choose a date shortly after payday so the money is invested before it can be spent.

How long should I invest monthly for?

Regular investing works best over long periods, generally at least five years and ideally much longer, giving markets time to recover from downturns.

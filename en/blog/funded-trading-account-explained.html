Funded Trading Account: How Prop Firm Challenges Really Work

Updated 2026-11-14 · 9 min read

A funded trading account is an arrangement in which a proprietary trading firm lets you trade its capital, usually after you pay a fee and pass an evaluation with strict profit and loss rules. If you pass, you keep a share of the profits you make, but one broken rule can end the account. This guide covers how the challenges work, what they cost, why so many traders fail and how to approach one sensibly.

What is a funded trading account?

A funded trading account is an account where a proprietary trading firm (a 'prop firm') provides the trading capital, or a simulated version of it, and you make the trading decisions. In return, the firm takes part of any profit, and you agree to follow a set of risk rules.

Most firms do not hand over capital straight away. You first pay a fee to take an evaluation, often called a challenge. You have to hit a profit target without breaching loss limits. If you pass, you get a funded account and can ask for payouts on the profits you make.

There is one detail many people miss. With many firms, the funded account is still a simulated or demo environment. Your trades may never reach the real market, and your payouts come from the firm's own money under its terms. The model is legitimate in principle, but you need to understand what you are buying: the chance to earn a profit share under strict conditions, not access to an ordinary brokerage account.

How a funded trading account challenge works

Details differ from firm to firm, but most evaluations share the same building blocks. The examples below use a hypothetical $100,000 account with round numbers so the maths is easy to follow.

Profit target

To pass, you need to reach a set profit, expressed as a percentage of the starting balance. If the target is 10% on a $100,000 account, you must reach $110,000 in closed profit without breaking any other rule. Two-step challenges often have a second phase with a lower target.

Daily drawdown limit

The daily loss limit caps how much you can lose in a single trading day. With a 5% daily limit on $100,000, you can lose at most $5,000 in a day. Many firms measure this from the balance or equity at the start of the day, and some count open (floating) losses as well as closed ones. That means a trade can breach the rule while it is still open, even if it would have recovered later.

Maximum (overall) drawdown: static vs trailing

The overall drawdown is the most the account may ever fall. It comes in two main forms:

  • Static drawdown: the floor is fixed. With a 10% static limit, the account can never drop below $90,000, however much profit you make first.
  • Trailing drawdown: the floor moves up as the account reaches new highs. Suppose a $50,000 account has a $2,500 trailing limit. If it rises to $52,000, the floor moves from $47,500 to $49,500. Profits you have made can therefore shrink the room you have to lose.

A trailing drawdown is usually harder to manage. Before you pay, check whether it trails on closed balance or on open equity, and whether it stops trailing at some point.

Minimum trading days and time limits

Many challenges require a minimum number of trading days, so a single lucky trade cannot pass the evaluation. Some also set a maximum period to reach the target, while others have no time limit. A deadline tends to push traders into taking more risk as it gets closer, which is worth keeping in mind.

One-step vs two-step evaluations

In a one-step challenge, you pass a single phase and then get funded. In a two-step challenge, you pass two phases in a row, usually with a lower target in the second. One-step formats often have tighter drawdown rules to make up for the shorter route.

Fees, resets and payout splits

The challenge fee is the main cost. It usually rises with account size. Some firms refund it with your first payout, while others never do. Many also sell resets or discounted retakes after a failed attempt, which makes it easy to keep paying again and again.

Once you are funded, profits are split between you and the firm. Advertised splits are often somewhere between 70% and 90% for the trader. The firm may also set a minimum payout amount, a waiting period before the first withdrawal or a set payout cycle. Read the payout terms as closely as the trading rules, because this is where firms differ most in practice.

Common rules that catch traders out

Beyond the headline limits, the small print often contains rules that end accounts unexpectedly:

  • News trading restrictions: no opening or closing trades in a window around major economic releases.
  • Weekend and overnight holding: some accounts must be flat before the weekend.
  • Consistency rules: no single day may make up more than a set share of your total profit.
  • Maximum lot size or leverage: caps on position size per instrument.
  • Prohibited strategies: bans on certain copy trading, hedging across accounts, exploiting price feed delays, or some automated systems.
  • Inactivity: the account may be closed if you do not trade for a set number of days.

Breaking any of these can lead to the account being cancelled or a payout being refused, even if you were profitable. Read the full rulebook before you pay, not after.

Why most people fail a funded account challenge

It is widely recognised in the industry that only a minority of people who attempt challenges go on to pass and receive payouts. The reasons tend to be behavioural rather than technical.

Oversized risk

A 10% target looks like it calls for big trades. Many traders risk 2% to 5% per trade to get there quickly. A short losing streak then breaks the daily or overall limit. A streak of losses is a normal statistical event, even for a sound strategy.

Overtrading

A time limit and a visible profit target encourage taking marginal setups, trading out of boredom, or trying to 'win back' a loss before the day ends. More trades with no edge simply means more chances to hit the drawdown.

Treating the fee as 'cheap'

Because the fee is small compared with the account size, it can feel like a lottery ticket. Traders who think 'I can always buy another one' tend to take risks they would never take with their own savings. Repeated fees add up to a real loss.

Not understanding the drawdown mechanics

Many accounts fail because the trader did not realise floating losses counted towards the daily limit, or that a trailing drawdown had moved up after a winning run.

No tested plan

Starting a challenge without a strategy that has been tested over many trades means the evaluation itself becomes the experiment, and an expensive one.

How to approach a funded trading account challenge

None of the following guarantees a pass. It does, however, address the most common reasons accounts fail.

Keep risk per trade far below the daily limit

Work backwards from the daily drawdown. On the hypothetical $100,000 account with a $5,000 daily limit:

  • Risking 2% ($2,000) per trade, three losses in a row ($6,000) breaches the daily limit.
  • Risking 0.5% ($500) per trade, it would take ten straight losses to reach the same limit, and the overall drawdown would allow twenty.

Smaller risk means the target takes longer to reach. That trade-off is the point: you are buying time for your edge, if you have one, to show up over enough trades.

Write a trading plan before you start

Put it in writing: which markets you trade, which setups qualify, where stops go, your risk per trade, when you will not trade (for example, around restricted news events) and the maximum number of trades per day. A written plan makes impulsive decisions easier to spot and to stop.

Set your own daily stop, tighter than the firm's

If the firm allows a $5,000 daily loss, you might stop for the day at $1,500 or after two losing trades, whichever comes first. A personal stop keeps one bad day from turning into a breached account.

Ignore the clock where possible

If you can, choose formats without a tight deadline. If there is a deadline, decide in advance that you will accept failing over increasing risk to catch up.

Treat the fee as money you can afford to lose

Plan your budget as if the fee will not come back. If losing it would cause financial strain, the challenge is not appropriate for you right now.

Risks and red flags when choosing a prop firm

The prop firm sector has grown quickly, and the quality of firms varies widely. This article does not recommend any firm. Instead, check for the following warning signs:

  • Vague or changing rules: terms that are hard to find, open to interpretation or changed without notice.
  • Unclear payout conditions: no clear timeline, high minimums or broad discretion to refuse withdrawals.
  • No clear company information: no registered legal entity, address or named management.
  • Pressure tactics: constant countdown discounts, aggressive affiliate marketing or promises of easy income.
  • Unrealistic marketing: claims that 'anyone' can be funded quickly, or screenshots of large payouts with no context.
  • Restrictions that look designed to fail you: very tight trailing drawdowns combined with short deadlines and many prohibited behaviours.
  • Regulatory status: many prop firms are not authorised as investment firms, so ordinary investor protections may not apply. Check your local regulator's warnings and register.

It is also worth asking how the firm makes its money. If its income depends mainly on challenge fees rather than on traders who succeed, its incentives may not line up with yours.

Is a funded trading account right for you?

A funded trading account can suit an experienced trader with a tested strategy, disciplined risk control and a fee they can genuinely afford to lose. It is a poor fit for someone hoping for a shortcut to income, someone still learning the basics, or someone who would feel pressure to keep buying resets.

Losing more than you intended is a real risk. It is just as likely to come through repeated fees as through trading losses. Be honest about your record before you start.

Frequently asked questions

What is a funded trading account?

It is an account where a proprietary trading firm provides the trading capital, often simulated, after you pay a fee and pass an evaluation. You trade under strict risk rules and keep a share of any profits.

How does a prop firm challenge work?

You pay a fee and must reach a profit target, for example 10%, without breaching a daily loss limit and a maximum drawdown limit. Many challenges also require a minimum number of trading days. Some have one phase and others have two.

Can you lose more than the challenge fee?

In most prop firm models you do not fund the trading losses yourself, so your direct loss is usually limited to the fees and resets you pay. Check each firm's terms to confirm this.

What is the difference between static and trailing drawdown?

A static drawdown has a fixed floor based on the starting balance. A trailing drawdown moves up as the account reaches new highs, which reduces the room you have to lose after profitable trades.

Why do most traders fail funded account challenges?

The usual causes are risking too much per trade, overtrading to hit the target quickly, misunderstanding how the drawdown rules work and treating the fee as cheap enough to gamble with.

Are prop firms regulated?

Many prop firms are not authorised as investment firms, so standard investor protections may not apply. Check the firm's legal entity and your local regulator's register and warning lists before paying.

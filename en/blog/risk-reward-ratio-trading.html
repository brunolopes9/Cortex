Risk-reward ratio: what it is and how to calculate it in trading

Updated 2026-11-21 · 6 min read

The risk-reward ratio compares how much you could lose on a trade with how much you could make if it plays out as planned. A 1:2 ratio means risking 1 euro to try to make 2. On its own it does not tell you whether a strategy works: you need to read it alongside the win rate and trading costs.

The risk-reward ratio is one of the first calculations a trader should do before opening a position. It answers a simple question: if I am wrong, how much do I lose, and if I am right, how much do I make? This guide explains what the ratio is, how to calculate it, what win rate you need to break even and where the measure falls short.

What is the risk-reward ratio?

It is the relationship between a trade's potential loss and its potential gain. The potential loss is set by the distance between your entry price and your stop loss. The potential gain is the distance between your entry and your profit target, often called the take profit.

It is usually written as 1:X. A 1:2 ratio means that for every euro at risk, the trade aims to make two. A 1:1 ratio means risking the same amount you hope to gain. Some platforms and books flip the order and quote a reward-to-risk figure (2:1), so always check which number represents the risk.

How to calculate the risk-reward ratio

The formula is straightforward:

  • Risk = entry price − stop loss price (for a long trade)
  • Reward = target price − entry price (for a long trade)
  • Ratio = reward ÷ risk

For a short trade the logic is identical, with the distances measured in the opposite direction.

A simple example (hypothetical figures)

Say you buy a share at 50 euros. You place your stop loss at 48 and your target at 54.

  • Risk per share: 50 − 48 = 2 euros
  • Reward per share: 54 − 50 = 4 euros
  • Ratio: 4 ÷ 2 = 2, so 1:2

Now imagine a hypothetical 10,000-euro account where you decide to risk no more than 1% per trade, or 100 euros. Since each share can lose 2 euros, you buy 50 shares (100 ÷ 2). If the stop is hit, you lose about 100 euros. If the target is hit, you make about 200 euros. This is where the ratio meets risk management: first decide how much you are willing to lose, then work out the position size.

What win rate do you need to break even?

This is the question that matters most. The higher the ratio, the less often you need to be right to break even on average. The break-even win rate is 1 ÷ (1 + R), where R is the reward per unit of risk.

Note that these figures exclude costs. Once you add spreads, commissions and slippage, the win rate you need always creeps a little higher.

Putting ratio and win rate together: expectancy

To see whether a strategy has an edge, combine both pieces into a single figure called expectancy:

Expectancy = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss)

Example over 20 trades (hypothetical)

Using the earlier setup (100 euros at risk, 200-euro target) and a 40% win rate:

  • 8 winning trades × 200 euros = 1,600 euros
  • 12 losing trades × 100 euros = 1,200 euros
  • Result before costs: +400 euros, or +20 euros per trade

Now add a hypothetical cost of 15 euros per trade. Over 20 trades that is 300 euros, and the result drops to +100 euros. If the win rate slips to 30%, the picture changes completely: 6 × 200 = 1,200 euros against 14 × 100 = 1,400 euros, which is −200 euros before costs.

The takeaway is clear: an attractive ratio on paper guarantees nothing if the real win rate is low or costs are high.

What is a good risk-reward ratio?

There is no magic number. You will often see 1:2 quoted as a benchmark, but the right ratio depends on the type of strategy:

  • Trend-following strategies tend to have high ratios (1:3 or more) and low win rates. They lose small and often, and win big but rarely.
  • Mean-reversion or short-term strategies tend to have lower ratios (1:1 or less) and higher win rates.

There is an unavoidable trade-off: moving the target further away raises the ratio, but it also makes it less likely that the price ever gets there. Picking a 1:5 ratio does not help if, in practice, the target is almost never reached.

Common mistakes

  • Moving the stop loss mid-trade to give it more room. That increases the risk and wipes out the ratio you planned.
  • Setting unrealistic targets just to make the ratio look good.
  • Ignoring costs, especially on short trades where the spread is large relative to the expected move.
  • Judging from too few trades. Ten trades tell you very little; a win rate only becomes meaningful over a large sample.
  • Forgetting position size. A 1:3 ratio on an oversized position can still do serious damage to an account.

Risks and limits of the measure

The risk-reward ratio is a plan, not a guarantee. A stop loss is not always filled at the price you set: on unexpected news or at the market open, prices can gap and the loss can be larger than planned. Leverage magnifies this effect.

Figures from tests on historical data can also mislead, because it is easy to fit a strategy to the past. When assessing any strategy or software, look for complete track records that include the losing months, and check that simulated results are clearly separated from live ones. Finally, losing streaks happen even in strategies with positive expectancy. With a 40% win rate, six or seven losses in a row are entirely possible. Risking only a small percentage of the account on each trade helps you get through those stretches.

Summary

  • The risk-reward ratio compares a trade's potential loss with its potential gain.
  • You calculate it by dividing the distance to the target by the distance to the stop loss.
  • It only means something when combined with the win rate and costs.
  • There is no universal ideal ratio; it should match the strategy and be realistic.

Frequently asked questions

What does a 1:2 risk-reward ratio mean?

It means that for every euro you risk losing down to the stop loss, the trade aims to make two euros up to the profit target.

How do you calculate the risk-reward ratio?

Divide the distance between your entry and your profit target by the distance between your entry and your stop loss. A target 4 euros above and a stop 2 euros below gives a 1:2 ratio.

What win rate do you need with a 1:2 ratio?

Before costs, about 33.3%. Once spreads, commissions and slippage are included, the required win rate is a little higher.

Does a high risk-reward ratio guarantee profits?

No. If the real win rate is low or costs are high, a strategy with a high ratio can still lose money. Stop losses can also be filled at a worse price than planned.

What is the best risk-reward ratio?

There is no single ideal figure. Trend-following strategies use higher ratios with fewer winners; short-term strategies use lower ratios with more winners. What matters is that the combination has positive expectancy after costs.

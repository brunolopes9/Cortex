Compound Interest Explained: Formula, Worked Examples and the Maths of Losses

Updated 2026-11-11 · 9 min read

Compound interest means you earn returns on your original money and also on the returns it has already made. Over time, growth builds on itself. This guide covers the formula, monthly contributions and the effect of time, and shows why losses hurt more than gains help.

What is compound interest?

Compound interest is interest calculated on your original sum plus any interest already added to it. Simple interest is different: it is paid only on the original amount. With compounding, each period's growth becomes part of the base for the next period, so the balance grows faster as time goes on.

The idea works in both directions. It helps savers and investors when returns are positive. It works against borrowers on credit cards and loans. And it makes losses especially costly, as we will see later.

Simple vs compound interest: a quick example

Imagine you put a hypothetical £10,000 somewhere that pays 5% a year for 10 years.

  • Simple interest: you earn £500 every year, which is 5% of £10,000. After 10 years you have £15,000.
  • Compound interest: in the first year you earn £500. In the second year you earn 5% of £10,500, which is £525, and so on. After 10 years you have about £16,289.

That gap of roughly £1,289 is interest earned on interest. Over short periods it looks small. Over decades it becomes the main driver of growth.

The compound interest formula

The standard compound interest formula is:

A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n × t)

  • A = the final amount
  • P = the principal (your starting sum)
  • r = the annual interest rate as a decimal (5% = 0.05)
  • n = how many times interest is compounded per year (1 for annually, 12 for monthly)
  • t = the number of years

If interest is compounded once a year, the formula reduces to A = P × (1 + r)^t.

Worked example: annual compounding

£10,000 at 5% a year, compounded annually, for 10 years:

A = 10,000 × (1.05)^10 = 10,000 × 1.6289 ≈ £16,289

Does compounding frequency matter?

It does, but less than most people expect. Here is the same £10,000 at 5% for 10 years, this time compounded monthly:

A = 10,000 × (1 + 0.05/12)^120 ≈ £16,470

That is about £181 more than annual compounding. More frequent compounding helps a little. The rate itself and the length of time matter far more. This is why savings products quote an AER (annual equivalent rate) or APY: it turns different compounding frequencies into one comparable annual figure.

Compound interest with monthly contributions

Most people don't invest a single lump sum and leave it alone. They add money regularly. When you make regular contributions, each payment begins compounding from the date it goes in.

The future value of a series of equal monthly contributions is:

FV = PMT × [((1 + i)^N − 1) / i]

  • PMT = the monthly contribution
  • i = the monthly rate (annual rate ÷ 12)
  • N = the total number of months

Worked example: £200 a month for 20 years

Suppose you invest a hypothetical £200 a month for 20 years at a steady 5% a year, compounded monthly. In practice, investment returns are never this steady.

  • Monthly rate: 0.05 ÷ 12 ≈ 0.004167
  • Number of months: 240
  • (1.004167)^240 ≈ 2.7126
  • FV ≈ 200 × (1.7126 ÷ 0.004167) ≈ £82,200

You would have paid in £48,000. The remaining roughly £34,200 comes from compounding. Because of the way the arithmetic works, a large share of that growth arrives in the final years, when the balance is at its biggest.

The effect of time: why starting early matters

Time is the strongest lever in the compound interest formula because it sits in the exponent. Compare two hypothetical savers. Both invest £200 a month at a steady 5% a year until age 65.

Saver A2540£96,000£305,000
Saver B3530£72,000£166,000

Saver A pays in only £24,000 more than Saver B, yet ends up with around £139,000 more. Those extra ten years at the start give the early contributions the longest time to compound.

The point is not that someone who starts late should give up. It is that time and consistency often matter more than trying to find an unusually high rate.

The rule of 72

For a quick mental estimate of how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the annual rate in percent:

  • At 3%: about 24 years
  • At 6%: about 12 years
  • At 9%: about 8 years

It is an approximation. At 5%, for example, the rule gives 14.4 years and the exact answer is about 14.2. It is still a useful way to see how strongly the rate affects doubling time.

The effect of losses: why −50% needs +100%

This is the side of compounding that often gets left out. Losses also compound, and recovering from them takes a larger percentage gain than the percentage lost.

The reason is simple. After a loss, you are working from a smaller base. If £10,000 falls by 50%, you have £5,000. To get back to £10,000, that £5,000 has to double, which is a 100% gain.

The general formula for the gain needed to recover is: required gain = 1 ÷ (1 − loss) − 1.

LossGain needed to break even

−20%+25%
−30%+42.9%
−50%+100%
−75%+300%
−90%+900%

Small losses are fairly easy to recover from. Large ones get much harder very quickly. That is why many professional approaches give limiting drawdowns (peak-to-trough falls) the same priority as chasing returns.

Volatility drag: when the average misleads

Averages can hide what compounding actually does to a balance. Take a hypothetical £10,000 that gains 50% one year and loses 50% the next:

  • After year one: £15,000
  • After year two: £7,500

The simple average return is 0%, yet you have lost 25%. Now compare two portfolios over two years:

  • Steady: +5% and +5%, so 1.05 × 1.05 = 1.1025, or +10.25%
  • Volatile: +20% and −10%, so 1.20 × 0.90 = 1.08, or +8%

Both have an average return of 5% a year, but the steadier path ends up with more money. This effect is often called volatility drag. What matters for your wealth is the compound (geometric) return, not the simple average.

What is a realistic rate of return?

Compound interest calculators make it easy to type in an exciting number. The results only mean something if the rate is realistic and you account for risk.

Cash savings

Rates on savings accounts and deposits tend to follow central bank interest rates, so they rise and fall over time. Your capital is generally stable in nominal terms, but after inflation the real return can be low or even negative.

Bonds

Bonds usually aim to pay more than cash in exchange for some risk, such as interest rate changes or the issuer failing to pay. Their prices can fall, particularly when interest rates rise.

Shares and equity funds

Over long periods, diversified share investments have historically delivered higher returns than cash, but with much larger swings along the way, including falls of 30% or more in severe downturns. Past performance does not guarantee future results, and the returns are neither steady nor guaranteed.

Red flags: when the numbers are too good

Compounding also helps you test claims. A promise of 10% a month sounds modest until you compound it: 1.10^12 ≈ 3.14, which is a gain of about 214% in a single year. Over five years it would multiply money more than 300 times. Steady returns of that size are not realistic, and offers built on them should be treated with great caution.

Hidden factors that reduce compound growth

Fees

Costs compound as well. Take a hypothetical £10,000 growing at 6% a year for 30 years. It reaches about £57,400. With a 1% annual fee, the net rate falls to 5% and the result is about £43,200. That 1% fee costs roughly £14,200 over the period. When you compare products or services, look at the total ongoing costs.

Inflation

A nominal return of 5% when inflation is 3% gives a real return of roughly 2%. For long-term planning, real returns tell you more about what your money will actually buy.

Taxes

Tax on interest, dividends or gains can reduce what is left to compound. Tax-efficient accounts exist in many countries, and the rules depend on where you live.

Debt

High-interest debt compounds against you. Credit card balances at high annual rates can grow quickly if you only make minimum payments. Paying off expensive debt is often one of the most reliable financial 'returns' available.

Compound interest in automated and active strategies

The same maths applies to trading and automated strategies. A strategy that makes small, consistent gains while keeping drawdowns under control can end up ahead of one with higher but erratic returns, because large losses take so much recovery. When you assess any strategy, ask about the maximum drawdown, the consistency of results and the costs, as well as the headline return.

Key takeaways

  • Compound interest earns returns on both your original money and the returns it has already made.
  • The formula is A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n × t). Time sits in the exponent, which is why it has such a strong effect.
  • Regular monthly contributions combined with time can build a large share of the final value.
  • Losses compound too: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover.
  • Volatility, fees, inflation and taxes all reduce real compound growth.
  • Be sceptical of high, steady returns that are presented as certain.

Frequently asked questions

What is the formula for compound interest?

A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n × t), where P is the principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is the number of compounding periods per year and t is the number of years. With annual compounding it simplifies to A = P × (1 + r)^t.

How much is £10,000 worth after 10 years at 5% compound interest?

With annual compounding it grows to about £16,289. With monthly compounding it reaches about £16,470. These are hypothetical figures that assume a steady rate.

Why does a 50% loss need a 100% gain to recover?

Because the loss leaves a smaller base. £10,000 that falls 50% becomes £5,000, and £5,000 must double (gain 100%) to return to £10,000.

What is the rule of 72?

It is a quick estimate of how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the annual rate in percent. At 6% a year, money doubles in roughly 12 years.

Is monthly compounding much better than annual compounding?

Only slightly. At 5% over 10 years the difference on £10,000 is around £180. The rate, the time invested and the costs matter much more than how often interest is compounded.

What is a realistic compound return?

It depends on the asset and the risk. Cash tends to follow central bank rates. Shares have historically returned more over long periods but with large swings. Promises of high, steady monthly returns are a warning sign.

How to Invest in Bitcoin: Ways to Get Exposure, Risks and Position Sizing

Updated 2026-11-10 · 9 min read

If you want to know how to invest in bitcoin, there are four main routes: buying the coin directly, using exchange-traded products, trading derivatives such as CFDs, or using an automated trading strategy. Each one differs in cost, control, security and risk, and none of them removes bitcoin's sharp price swings. This guide explains how each route works, what can go wrong and how much of your money it might make sense to commit.

Bitcoin is a digital asset that runs on a public, decentralised network. Its supply is capped at 21 million coins, it trades around the clock and its price is set purely by supply and demand on exchanges worldwide. That combination makes it attractive to some investors and alarming to others. Before working out how to invest in bitcoin, it helps to be clear about what you actually want: ownership of the coin itself, simple price exposure inside a normal brokerage account, short-term trading, or a hands-off systematic approach.

How to invest in bitcoin: the four main routes

Broadly, there are four ways to get exposure to bitcoin's price. They are not equally suitable for everyone, and the right choice depends on your experience, the time you can spend and how much responsibility you want to take for security.

  1. Buying spot bitcoin on a crypto exchange or app, and optionally moving it to your own wallet.
  2. Exchange-traded products (ETFs, ETPs and ETNs) that track the bitcoin price and trade on a stock exchange.
  3. Derivatives such as CFDs, which let you speculate on price moves, often with leverage, without owning any bitcoin.
  4. Automated trading, where a rules-based system opens and closes positions on your behalf.

1. Buying spot bitcoin

Buying "spot" means purchasing actual bitcoin at the current market price. You open an account with a crypto exchange or a broker that offers crypto, verify your identity, deposit money and place an order. You do not need to buy a whole coin; bitcoin is divisible to eight decimal places, so you can buy a small fraction.

Advantages of owning bitcoin directly

  • You own the underlying asset, not a claim on it.
  • You can withdraw it to a wallet you control.
  • No ongoing management fee, unlike most funds.

Drawbacks of buying spot

  • You are responsible for security, or you rely on the exchange to look after your coins.
  • Trading fees and spreads can be higher on some consumer apps than they first appear.
  • Exchanges have failed or been hacked in the past, and customer funds have been lost.

A tip that applies on any platform: compare the total cost, not only the advertised commission. A platform with "zero fees" may build its margin into a wider buy/sell spread.

Custody and security: who holds the keys?

With bitcoin, ownership is controlled by private keys. Whoever controls the keys controls the coins. This is why custody is one of the most important decisions when you invest in bitcoin directly.

Leaving bitcoin on an exchange

This is the simplest option. The exchange holds the keys and you see a balance in your account. The risk is counterparty risk: if the exchange is hacked, mismanaged or becomes insolvent, you may not get your coins back. Crypto holdings are generally not covered by the deposit-protection schemes that apply to bank accounts.

Self-custody with a wallet

You can move your bitcoin to a software wallet (an app) or a hardware wallet (a small physical device that keeps keys offline). Self-custody removes exchange risk but adds personal responsibility:

  • If you lose your recovery phrase and your device, your bitcoin is usually gone for good.
  • If someone else obtains your recovery phrase, they can take your coins, and transactions cannot be reversed.
  • Phishing sites, fake wallet apps and "support" scammers are common.

Basic security checklist

  • Use strong, unique passwords and app-based two-factor authentication rather than SMS where possible.
  • Write your recovery phrase on paper or metal and store it offline; never type it into a website or share it with anyone.
  • Buy hardware wallets only from the manufacturer or an authorised seller.
  • Send a small test amount before moving a large balance.
  • Be sceptical of anyone promising guaranteed returns or asking you to send crypto first.

2. Bitcoin ETFs, ETPs and ETNs

Exchange-traded products let you gain bitcoin price exposure through an ordinary investment account, just like buying a share. Spot bitcoin ETFs began trading in the United States in January 2024, and similar exchange-traded products have been listed in Europe for several years. Availability for retail investors varies by country and by platform, so check what your broker offers and what your local rules allow.

Why some investors prefer bitcoin ETPs

  • No need to manage private keys or wallets.
  • Can sometimes be held in existing brokerage or tax-advantaged accounts, depending on local rules.
  • Familiar process: buy and sell during market hours like any listed security.

Points to check

  • Ongoing charges: funds charge an annual fee, which reduces returns over time.
  • Structure: some products hold physical bitcoin; others use futures or are debt instruments (ETNs) that carry issuer risk.
  • Trading hours: bitcoin trades 24/7, but the ETP only trades when the stock exchange is open, so prices can gap at the open.
  • No coin ownership: you cannot withdraw bitcoin from the product.

3. Bitcoin CFDs and other derivatives

A contract for difference (CFD) is an agreement with a broker to exchange the difference in an asset's price between opening and closing a position. You never own bitcoin; you are speculating on its price, and you can go long (betting on a rise) or short (betting on a fall).

How leverage magnifies gains and losses

CFDs are usually traded on margin. Take a hypothetical example with 2:1 leverage: you deposit £1,000 and open a £2,000 position. If bitcoin rises 10%, the position gains £200, which is 20% of your deposit. If bitcoin falls 10%, you lose £200, also 20% of your deposit. A 50% fall would wipe out your entire margin. Given how quickly bitcoin can move, leveraged positions can be closed out very fast.

Costs and restrictions

  • Spreads and overnight financing charges make CFDs expensive to hold for long periods.
  • In the UK, the FCA has banned the sale of crypto derivatives, including CFDs, to retail consumers since January 2021.
  • In the EU, retail leverage on crypto CFDs is capped at 2:1, and brokers must display the percentage of retail accounts that lose money, which is typically a majority.

CFDs are generally better suited to experienced traders who understand margin and actively manage risk, not to someone looking to build long-term exposure.

4. Automated bitcoin trading

Automated trading uses predefined rules, executed by software, to enter and exit positions. A strategy might follow trends, react to volatility or rebalance at set intervals. The appeal is discipline: the system follows its rules without fear or greed, and it can monitor a market that never closes.

What to understand before using an automated strategy

  • No strategy wins all the time. Systems that worked in past conditions can underperform or lose money when markets change.
  • Backtests are not guarantees. Historical simulations can be over-fitted to past data.
  • Custody still matters. Ask where your money is held and who can withdraw it.
  • Fees and incentives. Understand whether you pay a fixed fee, a subscription or a share of profits.

Some services are structured so that funds stay in the client's own broker account while the strategy places trades; Cortex Automation, for example, works this way on a profit-share model. Even with that structure, trading losses remain possible and you should only allocate money you can afford to lose.

Understanding bitcoin volatility

Bitcoin is far more volatile than most mainstream assets. Over its history it has experienced several drawdowns where the price fell by well over half from a peak, and moves of 10% or more within days are not unusual. Prices can be driven by regulation, large holders, liquidity shifts, sentiment and broader market conditions.

Volatility cuts both ways. It is the reason some investors have seen large gains, and also the reason others have sold in panic at a loss. Ask yourself honestly: if your holding halved in value, would you hold, sell, or lose sleep? Your answer should shape how much you invest.

Lump sum or regular purchases?

Some investors use pound-cost averaging, buying a fixed amount at regular intervals (for example, £100 a month). This does not prevent losses, but it reduces the risk of putting everything in at a single high point and helps take emotion out of timing decisions. Remember that each purchase may carry a fee, so check costs for small, frequent buys.

Position sizing: how much should you put into bitcoin?

Position sizing is arguably more important than which route you choose. A common principle is to size a high-risk holding so that even a severe fall would not damage your overall finances.

A simple worked example

Imagine a hypothetical portfolio of £10,000:

  • With 5% in bitcoin (£500), a 50% fall costs £250, or 2.5% of the whole portfolio.
  • With 30% in bitcoin (£3,000), the same fall costs £1,500, or 15% of the portfolio.

The asset is identical; only the position size differs. Smaller positions make it easier to stay calm and stick to a plan.

Practical sizing guidelines

  • Build an emergency fund and clear high-interest debt first.
  • Never invest money you will need in the short term.
  • Avoid borrowing to buy bitcoin.
  • Decide in advance whether and when you will rebalance, for example back to a target percentage once a year.
  • If using leverage, size the position by how much you could lose, not by how much margin is required.

Bitcoin and tax: the general picture

Tax treatment varies widely between countries, so treat this as a general overview only. In many jurisdictions, including the UK, bitcoin is treated as a property-like asset rather than currency, and gains on disposal may be subject to capital gains tax.

A "disposal" often includes more than selling for cash. Depending on local rules, swapping bitcoin for another crypto, spending it or gifting it may also count. Income from trading activity, or from certain crypto rewards, may be taxed differently. ETFs and ETPs may be taxed like other securities, and CFD profits may have their own treatment.

  • Keep records of every purchase, sale and transfer, including dates, amounts, values in your home currency and fees.
  • Exchanges may share information with tax authorities, so assume transactions are visible.
  • If your situation is complex, speak to a qualified tax adviser in your country.

Step-by-step: getting started sensibly

  1. Define your goal: long-term holding, simple exposure or active trading.
  2. Choose your route: spot, ETP, derivatives or an automated strategy, based on the comparison above.
  3. Check the provider: look at its regulatory status in your country, track record, fees and how client funds are held.
  4. Set your position size before you buy, and write it down.
  5. Secure your accounts with two-factor authentication and, if self-custodying, a safely stored recovery phrase.
  6. Start small and learn how the platform, fees and withdrawals work.
  7. Keep records for tax from day one.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Buying after a sharp rally because of fear of missing out.
  • Using high leverage without understanding margin calls.
  • Keeping recovery phrases in screenshots, emails or cloud notes.
  • Trusting social-media "experts" or schemes promising fixed returns.
  • Ignoring fees, which can quietly erode returns on frequent trades.

Final thoughts

There is no single best answer to how to invest in bitcoin. Spot ownership offers direct control but demands care with security; ETPs offer convenience at the cost of ongoing fees; CFDs add leverage and risk; automated strategies offer discipline but still carry market and model risk. Whatever route you choose, sensible position sizing and good security habits matter more than trying to time the market.

Frequently asked questions

Is bitcoin a good investment for beginners?

Bitcoin is highly volatile and can fall sharply, so it is generally considered high risk. Beginners who choose to invest often keep the position small, avoid leverage and use simple routes such as buying spot or a regulated exchange-traded product where available.

How much money do I need to start investing in bitcoin?

Very little. Bitcoin is divisible to eight decimal places, so many platforms let you buy a small fraction. Check minimum order sizes and fees, as fixed fees can be expensive on very small purchases.

Is it safer to keep bitcoin on an exchange or in a wallet?

Each has risks. An exchange exposes you to hacks or insolvency, while self-custody means you are fully responsible for your keys and recovery phrase. A hardware wallet with a securely stored recovery phrase removes exchange risk but requires care.

Do I pay tax on bitcoin?

In many countries, including the UK, gains when you sell, swap or spend bitcoin may be subject to capital gains tax, and some activity may be taxed as income. Rules vary by country, so keep detailed records and check local guidance.

What is the difference between a bitcoin ETF and buying bitcoin?

Buying bitcoin gives you the asset itself, which you can move to your own wallet. A bitcoin ETF or ETP gives price exposure through a brokerage account without managing keys, but charges an annual fee and you cannot withdraw coins.

Can I trade bitcoin CFDs in the UK?

No, not as a retail client. The FCA has banned the sale of crypto derivatives, including CFDs, to retail consumers in the UK since January 2021.

New ECB president: Hernández de Cos to succeed Lagarde. What changes for you?

Updated 2026-11-28 · 6 min read

Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos has been chosen as the new ECB president and is expected to take over from Christine Lagarde in February 2027. In the short term, your mortgage payment will not change because of this choice: Euribor follows interest rate decisions and market expectations, not the name at the top.

The European Central Bank has picked its new ECB president. On Thursday, 26 November 2026, European leaders agreed that Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos will succeed Christine Lagarde at the head of the institution that sets interest rates for the euro area. French President Emmanuel Macron announced the choice, which still has to go through the formal steps set out in the EU treaties.

If you have a variable-rate mortgage, savings or investments, the practical question is whether this moves Euribor and your monthly payment. The short answer is: not for now. Here is why, what could change over time, and what is worth watching.

Who is the new ECB president?

Pablo Hernández de Cos is a 55-year-old economist from Madrid. He was governor of the Bank of Spain from 2018 to 2024 and currently runs the Bank for International Settlements in Basel, often described as the central bank for central banks.

He will be the first Spaniard to lead the ECB. He was preferred over the Dutch candidate Klaas Knot. The same package also named Laurence Boone and Markus Brunnermeier for the ECB's Executive Board.

At the Bank of Spain he dealt with the pandemic, Russia's invasion of Ukraine, rising public debt and the return of high inflation. International media describe him as a consensus builder.

When does he take office, and what happens next?

According to published reports, Hernández de Cos is expected to start in February 2027. Before that, the usual EU procedure applies:

  1. The European Parliament holds a hearing and gives its opinion.
  2. The ECB's Governing Council is consulted.
  3. EU heads of state and government formally make the appointment.

The ECB president serves a single eight-year term that cannot be renewed. The choice made now will therefore shape European monetary policy for much of the next decade.

Does a new ECB president change Euribor?

Not directly. ECB interest rates are not set by one person. They are voted on by the Governing Council, which brings together the Executive Board and the governors of the national central banks of the euro area countries.

The president matters: he chairs the meetings, explains the decisions to the public and helps build agreement. But the ECB's mandate stays the same: keeping inflation stable at around 2% over the medium term. That goal, and how prices actually evolve, drives the decisions.

Euribor is a market rate. It reflects what banks expect the ECB to do in the coming months. If markets think a new president will be more or less inclined to raise rates, expectations may shift slightly, but inflation and economic data carry far more weight.

Where is Euribor now?

On Friday, 27 November, three-month Euribor was fixed at 2.791%, six-month at 3.063% and twelve-month at 3.341%, according to figures reported by the financial press. The ECB raised rates in September, held them at its 28 October meeting and meets again on 16 and 17 December, with Christine Lagarde still in charge.

Example: how much does a mortgage payment move if rates change?

Since the appointment does not change rates on its own, what matters is the effect of a possible future ECB decision. Here is a hypothetical example with round numbers.

Take a 150,000 euro mortgage over 30 years at a total rate (Euribor plus margin) of 4%. The monthly payment is roughly 715 euros. If the total rate rises by 0.25 percentage points to 4.25%, the payment goes to about 740 euros, around 20 to 25 euros more per month. A cut of the same size would save a similar amount.

This is for illustration only. The real effect depends on the outstanding balance, the remaining term, your bank's margin and when your contract resets the rate.

Why do markets watch the new ECB president so closely?

Investors try to anticipate how the central bank will respond to inflation and growth. The president's communication, in press conferences and speeches, helps markets read that direction.

That is why Hernández de Cos's first appearances, including his hearing at the European Parliament, will be closely followed. Comments on inflation, energy or financial stability can trigger short-term moves in bonds, the euro and Euribor. That is normal and does not, on its own, signal a change of course.

What are the risks to keep in mind?

  • Variable rates: borrowers on variable rates remain exposed to ECB decisions, whoever leads the bank.
  • Early labels: calling a central banker hawkish or dovish before he takes office rarely says much about future decisions.
  • Short-term volatility: institutional news can cause quick market swings that do not always last.
  • Inflation: it is the main driver of rate decisions and can surprise in either direction.

What should you do now?

For most households, this news does not call for any immediate action. A few sensible habits apply in any rate environment:

  • Know which index your mortgage follows and when the next reset is due.
  • Keep some room in your budget for a moderate rise in your payment.
  • Compare deposit rates rather than leaving cash idle with no return.
  • Avoid investment decisions based on headlines alone.

For now, the 16–17 December decision, the ECB's new economic projections and early 2027 inflation data matter more to your finances than the change of president.

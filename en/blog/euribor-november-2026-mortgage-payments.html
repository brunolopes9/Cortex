Euribor in November 2026: how much are mortgage payments rising?

Updated 2026-11-14 · 6 min read

The 12-month Euribor closed at 3.328% on 13 November 2026, and this month's resets are pushing up payments on variable-rate home loans. In a DECO Proteste simulation for Portugal, a 150,000 euro mortgage linked to the 12-month Euribor now costs about 89 euros a month more than a year ago.

Euribor in November 2026 is sitting at levels many borrowers had not seen for a while. The 12-month rate closed above 3.3% again on 13 November, and anyone with a variable-rate mortgage linked to Euribor is feeling it in their monthly payment. Below we look at where rates stand, how much payments are rising, what the ECB (European Central Bank) decided and what could happen before the end of the year.

Where does Euribor stand in November 2026?

On Friday, 13 November, the 12-month Euribor rose to 3.328%, up 0.035 percentage points from the previous day. The 3-month rate edged up to 2.775%, while the 6-month rate slipped slightly to 3.015%.

For your mortgage, though, a single day rarely matters. Most contracts use the monthly average of the month before the reset date. October's averages, which apply to resets taking place in November, were:

TenorRate on 13 November 2026October 2026 average
3-month Euribor2.775%2.635%
6-month Euribor3.015%2.975%
12-month Euribor3.328%3.239%

The October average for the 12-month rate was roughly one percentage point higher than in October 2025. That gap is the main reason why borrowers whose loans reset once a year are seeing the biggest jumps.

How much are mortgage payments rising in November?

Portugal offers a clear example. According to simulations by the consumer association DECO Proteste, published by the Lusa news agency at the end of October, a 150,000 euro loan over 30 years with a 1% margin (spread) looks like this after the November reset:

  • 12-month Euribor: the payment rises to 736.94 euros, 89.31 euros more than at the November 2025 reset.
  • 6-month Euribor: up 44.24 euros compared with the May reset, to 713.96 euros.
  • 3-month Euribor: up 17.62 euros compared with August, to 684.92 euros.

The reason for the difference is simple. A loan that resets every 12 months is being compared with the rate of a year ago, when Euribor was much lower. A loan that resets every 3 months absorbs increases gradually, in smaller steps.

This affects a lot of households. According to Banco de Portugal data cited by ECO, around 40% of Portuguese variable-rate home loans are linked to the 6-month Euribor, about 31% to the 12-month rate and about 24% to the 3-month rate. Borrowers in other euro area countries with Euribor-linked loans face the same logic.

Why is Euribor going up?

Euribor is the benchmark rate at which euro area banks lend to each other. It follows the ECB's policy rates closely and, above all, what markets expect the ECB to do over the coming months.

After eight rate cuts that began in June 2024, the ECB started raising rates again in 2026: by 0.25 points in June, its first hike since September 2023, and by another 0.25 points in September. The underlying concern is inflation, with rising energy prices seen as the main risk.

There is also a telling detail in the table above. The 12-month rate is higher than the 6-month rate, which in turn is higher than the 3-month rate. When longer tenors pay more, it usually means the market is pricing in higher rates ahead.

What did the ECB decide, and what could happen in December?

At its meeting on 29 October, the ECB left its three key interest rates unchanged: 2.50% for the deposit facility, 2.65% for the main refinancing operations and 2.90% for the marginal lending facility. According to the Portuguese newspaper Observador, ECB President Christine Lagarde said a hike had been discussed at that meeting.

The next monetary policy meeting is on 17 December. Markets and several analysts see room for another 0.25-point increase then, but that is an expectation, not a decision. The ECB keeps saying it decides meeting by meeting, based on inflation and economic data.

Example: what does each 0.25 points add to a payment?

To get a feel for the impact of a possible hike, here is a hypothetical example with round numbers: a 100,000 euro loan over 30 years, where the total rate (Euribor plus margin) goes from 4% to 4.25% and then to 4.5%.

In this example, every 0.25 points adds about 15 euros a month for each 100,000 euros owed, close to 180 euros a year. The exact figure depends on the outstanding balance, the remaining term and the margin in your contract.

I have a variable rate: what can I do?

There is no one-size-fits-all answer, but a few steps make sense for most people:

  • Know your next reset date. It is in your contract or in your bank's statements. That tells you which Euribor average will apply and gives you time to plan.
  • Stress-test your budget. Work out a payment slightly higher than today's and check that it still fits comfortably.
  • Talk to your bank. You can ask to renegotiate the margin or compare offers from other lenders to switch, keeping all the costs in mind.
  • Compare fixed or mixed rates. They swap uncertainty for predictability, but the starting rate may be higher. Always compare using the APR.
  • Consider an early repayment. Paying down part of the balance lowers the payment or shortens the term. Check any fee that applies and keep an emergency fund in place.

If payments start to become hard to manage, the most important thing is to contact your bank early. Most euro area countries have procedures to help borrowers before arrears build up, and they work best when used in time.

What about savers?

Higher rates have an upside for savers. Interest on term deposits and other savings products tends to follow, though usually with a delay and not always to the same extent. It is worth comparing offers rather than leaving money idle in a current account.

What are the risks and uncertainties?

Euribor moves every day and can fall as quickly as it rose. A different path for inflation, energy prices or the European economy could lead the ECB to change course. The simulations above show orders of magnitude; your actual payment always depends on your own contract.

It is also wise not to make rushed decisions based on a single day in the market. Switching from a variable to a fixed rate, for instance, is a long-term choice that deserves a calm comparison of several offers.

The bottom line

Euribor in November 2026 remains elevated, especially at 12 months, and this month's resets mean higher payments for many variable-rate borrowers. The ECB held rates in October, but markets see a possible hike in December. Knowing your contract, running the numbers and comparing alternatives is the calmest way through this phase.

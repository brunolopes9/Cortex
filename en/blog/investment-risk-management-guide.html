Investment Risk Management: How to Protect Your Capital Before Chasing Returns

Updated 2026-11-12 · 9 min read

Investment risk management is the set of rules that decides how much you can lose on any single position, and across your whole portfolio, before you ever think about profit. Get it right and a bad run becomes a setback rather than a disaster. This guide covers risk per trade, drawdown, stop losses, position sizing, risk/reward, diversification and discipline, with a full worked example.

Most people start investing or trading by asking how much they could make. Experienced investors tend to ask a different question first: how much could I lose, and could I survive it? That shift in thinking is the heart of investment risk management. It does not remove risk, because no method can, but it keeps losses within limits you have chosen in advance rather than limits the market chooses for you.

In this guide we break risk management down into practical, measurable parts. Each section answers a question people commonly search, and the numbers used are simple, round and purely hypothetical so you can follow the arithmetic.

What is investment risk management?

Investment risk management is the process of identifying, measuring and limiting the potential losses in your portfolio. In practice it means having written rules for:

  • How much capital you put at risk on each trade or investment.
  • Where you will exit if the market moves against you.
  • How large each position is relative to your account.
  • How much total decline (drawdown) you can tolerate before you stop and reassess.
  • How spread out your exposure is across assets, sectors and strategies.

The aim is not to avoid every loss. Losses are a normal part of investing. The aim is to make sure no single loss, or string of losses, can do damage you cannot recover from.

Why losses hurt more than gains help

The most important idea in risk management is that losses and gains are not symmetrical. If your account falls by a percentage, you need a larger percentage gain to get back to where you started.

Loss on accountGain needed to recover
10%11.1%
20%25%
30%42.9%
50%100%
75%300%

A 50% loss requires you to double your money just to break even. This is why protecting capital usually matters more than squeezing out extra return. Small, controlled losses are recoverable; large ones can take years, or never be recovered.

Risk per trade: how much should you risk?

Risk per trade is the amount you stand to lose if a position hits your exit point, expressed as a percentage of your total account. It is not the size of the position. You could hold a £5,000 position but only risk £100 on it if your stop loss is close to your entry.

Many traders use a fixed rule such as risking 0.5% to 2% of the account per trade. There is nothing magical about these figures, but they illustrate a principle: keep each loss small enough that a losing streak does not become ruinous.

What a losing streak looks like at different risk levels

Even a sound strategy can lose many times in a row. The table below shows, hypothetically, what ten consecutive losses would do to an account at different fixed risk levels (each loss taken as a percentage of the remaining balance).

At 1% risk, ten losses is an uncomfortable dip. At 10% risk, it is close to wiping out the account. The strategy is identical in both cases; only the risk management differs.

Drawdown and maximum drawdown explained

Drawdown is the decline from a peak in your account value to a subsequent low, usually shown as a percentage. Maximum drawdown (often written MDD) is the largest such peak-to-trough fall over a given period.

How to calculate maximum drawdown

Suppose a hypothetical account grows from £10,000 to £12,000, then falls to £9,000 before recovering. The drawdown is measured from the highest point, not the starting point:

  • Peak: £12,000
  • Trough: £9,000
  • Fall: £3,000
  • Maximum drawdown: £3,000 ÷ £12,000 = 25%

Notice that the account is only 10% below its starting value, yet the investor experienced a 25% fall from the top. That is the figure that tests nerves and patience.

Why maximum drawdown matters

Maximum drawdown tells you two things. First, how much pain a strategy has historically involved. Second, whether you could realistically have stayed with it. A strategy with attractive average returns is of little use if its drawdowns are so deep that you would abandon it at the worst moment. Remember too that a past maximum drawdown is not a ceiling: future drawdowns can be larger.

A useful habit is to set your own drawdown limit, for example pausing new trades and reviewing your approach if the account falls 10% or 15% from its peak.

Stop loss orders: defining your exit in advance

A stop loss is a pre-set level at which you exit a losing position. It turns a vague intention ("I'll sell if it gets bad") into a concrete rule.

Types of stop loss

  • Fixed stop: placed at a set price, often below a recent low or support level.
  • Percentage stop: placed a fixed percentage below your entry.
  • Volatility-based stop: distance based on how much the asset typically moves, so you are not stopped out by normal noise.
  • Trailing stop: moves up as the price rises, locking in part of a gain while still limiting loss.

Limitations of stop losses

Stop losses are not a guarantee. In fast markets, or when prices gap overnight or over a weekend, your order may be filled at a worse price than the stop level. This is called slippage. Some brokers offer guaranteed stops for a fee, but standard stops can and do slip. Placing a stop too close can also get you knocked out repeatedly by ordinary price fluctuations.

Position sizing: linking risk to trade size

Position sizing is where risk per trade and stop loss come together. The basic formula is:

Position size = Amount you are willing to risk ÷ Risk per unit

where risk per unit is the distance between your entry price and your stop loss. This approach means that wider stops lead to smaller positions, and tighter stops allow larger ones, while the money at risk stays the same.

Risk/reward ratio: is the trade worth taking?

The risk/reward ratio compares what you could lose with what you aim to gain. If you risk £100 to target £200, the ratio is 1:2.

The ratio links directly to how often you need to be right. Ignoring costs, the break-even win rate is 1 ÷ (1 + reward multiple):

Risk/rewardBreak-even win rate (before costs)
1:150%
1:233.3%
1:325%

A higher reward target lowers the win rate you need, but it also tends to be hit less often. There is no free lunch: the ratio only helps if your targets are realistic for the market you trade. Fees, spreads and slippage also raise the real break-even point.

Worked example: putting the numbers together

Here is a complete, hypothetical example combining the ideas above.

  1. Account size: £10,000.
  2. Risk rule: 1% per trade, so maximum loss on this trade is £100.
  3. Entry price: £50 per share.
  4. Stop loss: £48, placed below a recent low. Risk per share is £2.
  5. Position size: £100 ÷ £2 = 50 shares, a position worth £2,500.
  6. Target: £54. Potential gain is £4 per share, or £200 in total.
  7. Risk/reward: £100 risk to £200 reward, a ratio of 1:2.

Now imagine running this same plan over ten trades, winning four and losing six. Ignoring costs and assuming fixed £100 risk:

  • Four wins × £200 = £800
  • Six losses × £100 = −£600
  • Net result: +£200

Despite losing more often than winning, the hypothetical account ends slightly up, because losses were capped and winners were larger. Change one variable, for example letting a single loser run to £600 because the stop was removed, and the whole result turns negative. That is the practical value of risk management: the rules protect the outcome from individual mistakes.

This example is illustrative only. Real results depend on whether the targets and stops are actually reached, on costs and on market conditions, and they can be considerably worse.

Diversification: spreading risk across your portfolio

Risk per trade controls individual positions; diversification controls how those positions interact. Ten trades each risking 1% are not truly separate if they are all in closely related assets that tend to move together. In a sell-off they may all hit their stops at once, turning ten small risks into one large one.

Ways to diversify

  • Across asset classes: shares, bonds, cash, commodities and others behave differently.
  • Across sectors and regions: avoid concentrating everything in one industry or country.
  • Across strategies and time frames: combining approaches that do not depend on the same conditions.
  • Total exposure limits: cap the combined risk of all open positions, for example no more than 5% of the account at risk at any one time.

Diversification reduces the impact of any single holding going wrong, but it does not eliminate market-wide risk. In severe crises, correlations between assets often rise, and many things fall together.

Emotional discipline: the hardest part of risk management

Most risk management failures are not mathematical. They happen when people know the rules and break them. Common patterns include:

  • Moving a stop loss further away to avoid accepting a loss.
  • Revenge trading: increasing size after a loss to win it back quickly.
  • Overconfidence after a winning streak, leading to larger and riskier positions.
  • Fear of missing out, entering trades without a plan because prices are moving fast.
  • Closing winners too early while letting losers run, which undermines any risk/reward plan.

Practical ways to stay disciplined

  • Write your rules down before you trade, including risk per trade and drawdown limits.
  • Decide entry, stop and target before placing the order, not after.
  • Keep a trading journal recording each decision and whether you followed your plan.
  • Set a daily or weekly loss limit at which you stop trading.
  • Consider automation for execution. Rule-based systems apply the same position sizing and stops every time, which removes some in-the-moment emotional decisions. A simple investment risk management checklist

  • Do I know my maximum loss on this position in pounds and as a percentage?
  • Is my stop loss set, and is it based on a logical level rather than hope?
  • Is my position size calculated from my risk rule, not from gut feeling?
  • Does the potential reward justify the risk, including costs?
  • How much of my total account is at risk across all open positions?
  • Are my positions genuinely diversified, or all exposed to the same factor?
  • Have I hit my drawdown limit, and if so, have I paused to review?

Key risks to keep in mind

No risk management framework makes investing safe. Prices can gap through stops, liquidity can disappear, leverage can magnify losses beyond your initial stake on some products, and historical drawdowns do not cap future ones. Only invest money you can afford to lose, understand the products you use, and consider speaking to a qualified, independent adviser about your personal circumstances.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 1% rule in risk management?

The 1% rule means you risk no more than 1% of your total account on a single trade. On a £10,000 account that is a maximum loss of £100 per trade, set by your stop loss and position size. It is a guideline, not a guarantee, as slippage can cause larger losses.

How do you calculate maximum drawdown?

Take the highest account value (peak), subtract the lowest value reached afterwards (trough), and divide by the peak. For example, a fall from £12,000 to £9,000 is a £3,000 drop, or a 25% maximum drawdown.

What is a good risk/reward ratio?

Many traders look for at least 1:2, meaning the potential gain is twice the potential loss. At 1:2 you need to win about 33% of trades to break even before costs. The right ratio depends on whether your targets are realistic for the market you trade.

How do I calculate position size?

Divide the amount you are willing to risk by the distance between your entry and stop loss. Risking £100 with an entry at £50 and a stop at £48 gives £100 ÷ £2 = 50 shares.

Does a stop loss guarantee my maximum loss?

No. A standard stop loss becomes a market order when triggered, so in fast markets or price gaps it may be filled at a worse price. Some brokers offer guaranteed stops, usually for an extra cost.

Does diversification remove investment risk?

Diversification reduces the impact of any single investment performing badly, but it cannot remove market-wide risk. During major sell-offs many assets can fall at the same time.

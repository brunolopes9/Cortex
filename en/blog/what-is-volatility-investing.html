What is volatility? A plain guide for investors

Updated 2026-11-27 · 6 min read

Volatility measures how much and how quickly the price of an investment moves up and down. It does not tell you whether you will make or lose money, only how bumpy the ride is. This guide explains what volatility is, how it is calculated and why it can change your end result, using a simple example with round numbers.

If you are asking what is volatility, here is the short answer: it is a measure of how strongly the price of an asset swings over time. A low-volatility asset moves slowly within a narrow range. A high-volatility asset can rise or fall sharply in just a few days.

What is volatility in investing?

It helps to separate two ideas: direction and size. Volatility is only about size. One share can climb steadily with little volatility, while another can end the year exactly where it started after big swings in both directions.

In practice, volatility answers a very concrete question: how much could the value of my investment move in a normal day, month or year?

A simple example: same average, different outcome

Picture two hypothetical investments, A and B. Each starts with €1,000 and each has an average return of 5% a year over five years. The only difference is the path.

YearInvestment AInvestment B
1+6%+25%
2+4%-15%
3+5%+20%
4+7%-10%
5+3%+5%
Simple average5%5%
End value (approx.)€1,276€1,205

Both have the same average, yet B finishes about €70 behind. The reason is that losses hurt more than gains of the same size: after a 15% fall, you need a gain of roughly 18% just to get back to where you were.

This effect, sometimes called volatility drag, shows that volatility is not just an emotional nuisance. It can also eat into compound growth over the years.

How is volatility measured?

Standard deviation

The most common measure is the standard deviation of returns. Put simply, it shows how far returns tend to stray from their average. The higher the standard deviation, the more volatile the asset.

In the example above, A's returns always stay close to 5%, so its standard deviation is low (around 1.6 percentage points). B's returns range from -15% to +25%, so its standard deviation is much higher (around 18 points).

A rough rule of thumb: if an asset has annual volatility of 20% and an expected average return of 5%, then in about two years out of three the result would land somewhere between -15% and +25%. Real markets produce more extreme years than this rule implies, so treat it as an estimate, not a promise.

Daily versus annual volatility

Volatility is usually quoted on an annual basis so that assets can be compared. To turn daily volatility into annual volatility, multiply by the square root of the number of trading days in a year (about 252), which is roughly 16. An asset that moves about 1% a day has annual volatility of around 16%.

Historical and implied volatility

  • Historical volatility: calculated from past prices. It tells you what has already happened.
  • Implied volatility: derived from option prices. It reflects what the market expects over the coming weeks. The VIX index, for instance, tracks the implied volatility expected for the US S&P 500 over the next 30 days.

Neither one predicts which way prices will go. Both describe the size of moves, past or expected.

Is volatility the same as risk?

Not quite. Volatility is a widely used measure of risk, but it is not the whole story. For long-term investors, the risk that matters most is usually a permanent loss of capital, or needing the money at a moment when prices are down.

Even so, volatility matters for three reasons:

  • Time horizon: if you need the money within a year, a very volatile asset might be down at exactly that moment.
  • Behaviour: big swings push many people to sell in fear or buy in excitement, often at the worst time.
  • Leverage: with leveraged products, an ordinary swing can turn into a large loss of capital.

A useful companion measure is drawdown, the largest fall from a previous peak. Volatility tells you how much the price usually moves; drawdown tells you how far it fell at the worst point.

Which assets are the most volatile?

Levels change over time, but some broad patterns hold:

Short-term bonds from strong issuersLowSensitive to changes in interest rates
Broad stock market indicesMediumCan fall sharply in a crisis
Small-company sharesHighReact strongly to company news

Volatility is not fixed. An asset that has been calm for years can become very jumpy in a crisis, and many assets tend to fall together when markets are under stress.

How can investors deal with volatility?

  1. Start with your time horizon: money you will need soon should not sit in highly volatile assets.
  2. Size positions sensibly: a smaller position in a volatile asset can have the same impact on your portfolio as a larger one in a calm asset.
  3. Diversify: combining assets that do not always move together can reduce the overall volatility of a portfolio.
  4. Invest gradually: buying in stages reduces the impact of picking a bad moment.
  5. Set exit rules: in trading, stops based on volatility help avoid being shaken out by normal noise, but they do not remove losses.
  6. Avoid excessive leverage: it magnifies the effect of every swing.

Hypothetical example: if you are willing to risk €100 on a trade and the asset usually moves €2 a day, a stop €4 away from your entry allows a position of 25 units. For an asset that moves €0.50 a day, a stop €1 away would allow 100 units for the same risk.

Risks to keep in mind

  • Past volatility does not guarantee future volatility.
  • Measures based on averages tend to underestimate extreme moves.
  • Low volatility does not mean no risk: credit, liquidity and inflation risk can still apply.
  • With leveraged products such as CFDs, volatility can cause fast losses larger than expected.

Summary

Volatility measures how strongly prices swing, not which way they go. It helps you choose assets that suit your time horizon, size your positions and prepare for the bad months. Understanding what volatility is ranks among the first steps towards investing with realistic expectations.

Frequently asked questions

What is volatility in simple terms?

It is a measure of how much the price of an investment moves up and down over time. The bigger and more frequent the swings, the more volatile the asset.

Is high volatility good or bad?

Neither in itself. It can create opportunities but also sharp falls. For money you will need soon, highly volatile assets are usually a poor fit.

How do you calculate volatility?

The usual method is the standard deviation of returns, normally expressed on an annual basis. To go from daily to annual, multiply by about 16, the square root of roughly 252 trading days.

What is the VIX index?

It is an index of the implied volatility expected for the S&P 500 over the next 30 days, calculated from option prices. It does not indicate market direction.

Does high volatility mean I will lose money?

Not necessarily. It means the value can move more, both up and down. The risk of loss rises mainly if you have to sell at a bad moment or if you use leverage.

What Is an ETF? How It Works, What It Costs and the Risks

Updated 2026-11-28 · 6 min read

An ETF is an investment fund listed on a stock exchange that usually tracks an index, such as a basket of the world's largest companies. You buy it like a share, it spreads your money across many assets at a typically low cost, but it rises and falls with the market it follows.

What is an ETF? The short answer

ETF stands for Exchange Traded Fund. If you are wondering what is an ETF, the simplest answer is this: it is an investment fund that you buy and sell on a stock exchange, just like a share. Instead of backing a single company, each unit gives you a small slice of a whole basket of assets: dozens, hundreds or even thousands of shares, bonds or other holdings.

Most ETFs track an index, for example an index of the largest European or global companies, and simply aim to mirror its performance. They do not try to beat the market; they try to match it, in good years and bad ones. That is why ETFs are often described as passive investing.

How does an ETF work in practice?

An asset manager launches the fund, picks the index it wants to follow and buys the securities in that index, or a representative sample. The fund's units are then listed on an exchange. Investors buy them through a broker or bank during market hours, at the price available at that moment.

Three ideas are worth keeping in mind:

  • It follows an index: if the index rises 5%, the ETF should rise by roughly 5% before costs; if it falls 5%, so does the ETF.
  • It trades throughout the day: unlike a traditional mutual fund, which is priced once a day, an ETF has a live price while the exchange is open.
  • Its assets are ring-fenced: the holdings belong to the fund, not to the manager. That does not remove market risk, but it shields investors from some problems at the management company.

Physical or synthetic replication?

With physical replication, the ETF actually owns the shares or bonds in the index. With synthetic replication, it uses contracts with banks, known as swaps, to deliver the index return. Synthetic ETFs can be efficient, but they add the risk that the counterparty fails, even though rules and collateral are in place to limit it. For beginners, physical replication is usually easier to understand.

Accumulating or distributing?

Distributing ETFs pay dividends out to investors from time to time. Accumulating ETFs reinvest them automatically inside the fund. Which one suits you depends on whether you want regular income and on how each type is taxed where you live.

A simple example: €10,000 in an ETF

Imagine, purely hypothetically, that you invest €10,000 in an ETF tracking a global equity index, with an annual charge (TER) of 0.20%.

  • Approximate yearly fund cost: 0.20% × €10,000 = €20.
  • If the index gains 10% in a year, your holding grows to about €11,000, minus that cost.
  • If the index falls 20%, your holding drops to about €8,000. An ETF does not protect you from falls.

Now compare it with a hypothetical traditional fund charging 1.5% a year and achieving the same gross return. Assume the market returns 5% a year for 10 years (another made-up figure, used only for illustration):

Hypothetical scenarioNet yearly returnValue after 10 years
No costs5.0%about €16,290
ETF costing 0.20%4.8%about €15,980
Fund costing 1.5%3.5%about €14,110

The gap of almost €1,900 comes purely from fees. That is why the annual charge is one of the most important things to check when choosing an ETF. Remember, though, that markets never deliver a fixed return: there are good years, bad years and occasionally sharp falls.

How much does it cost to invest in an ETF?

Costs fall into three groups:

  • Fund costs: the TER (total expense ratio) is deducted daily from the fund's value. You never receive a bill; it shows up in the price.
  • Broker costs: trading commissions, any custody fees and currency conversion costs if the ETF trades in another currency.
  • Spread: the gap between the buying and selling price. It is usually tight for large, heavily traded ETFs and can be wider for niche ones.

Before investing, read the Key Information Document (KID). In a few pages it sets out the fund's objective, its costs and its risk level.

No option is right for everyone. People who enjoy researching companies one by one often prefer individual shares. Those looking for a simple, diversified way to follow the market tend to look at ETFs.

What are the risks of an ETF?

An ETF is diversified, but it is not a savings account. Your capital is not guaranteed. The main risks are:

  • Market risk: if the index falls, the ETF falls. In past crises, equity indices have lost more than 30% within a few months.
  • Currency risk: an ETF holding US shares depends on the dollar, even if it is quoted in euros or pounds.
  • Concentration risk: thematic or sector ETFs bet on a single theme or a small group of companies.
  • Tracking error: the ETF can drift slightly away from its index because of costs and the way it replicates.
  • Leveraged and inverse ETFs: they multiply or reverse the index's daily move. They are complex, short-term products and are not suitable for beginners.
  • Liquidity: small ETFs may have wider spreads and, in rare cases, may be closed by the manager.

How to choose an ETF: practical steps

  1. Set your goal and time horizon. Equities make more sense for long horizons; bonds tend to move less but carry their own risks.
  2. Pick the index. Global, European, single country, bonds? The index defines what you are really buying.
  3. Compare the TER and fund size. Larger funds tend to have tighter spreads.
  4. Check replication and dividend policy. Physical or synthetic, accumulating or distributing.
  5. Confirm your broker's fees. High commissions can cancel out the advantage of a cheap ETF, especially on small purchases.
  6. Consider investing regularly. A fixed monthly amount reduces the impact of buying at a bad moment, although it does not prevent losses.

Are ETFs worth it?

For many long-term investors, ETFs are a practical way to diversify at low cost. But they only make sense if your time horizon, your tolerance for losses and your goals line up. If you might need the money within a year or two, think carefully before putting it into equities, even through an ETF.

Tax treatment depends on your country and on the type of fund, so check the local rules on gains and dividends before you invest.

Summary

An ETF is an exchange-listed fund that tracks an index. It offers diversification, transparency and usually low costs, but it does not protect you from market falls. Before investing, look at the index, the TER, the replication method, currency risk and your broker's fees, and only invest money you will not need in the short term.

Frequently asked questions

What is an ETF in simple terms?

It is an investment fund traded on a stock exchange like a share that usually tracks an index, giving you exposure to many assets in a single purchase.

Can you lose money with an ETF?

Yes. An ETF follows the market, so if its index falls, the value of your investment falls too. Capital is not guaranteed.

How much does an ETF cost?

There is the fund's annual charge (TER), your broker's fees and the spread. In a hypothetical example with a 0.20% TER, the yearly fund cost on €10,000 would be about €20.

What is the difference between an ETF and a mutual fund?

An ETF trades on an exchange throughout the day and is usually cheaper and passively managed; a traditional mutual fund is bought through a bank or manager, priced once a day and often actively managed.

Accumulating or distributing ETF: which is better?

Distributing ETFs pay out dividends; accumulating ETFs reinvest them. The choice depends on whether you need regular income and on local tax rules.
